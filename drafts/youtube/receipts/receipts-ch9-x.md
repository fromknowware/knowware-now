### 2. Receipts

1. **Program dates (Turner).** Turner's prepared statement calls MKULTRA "a closely held CIA project conducted from 1953-1964" (CIA PDF p. 10). In oral testimony he says it "took place from 1953 to 1964," and that the 1963 Inspector General report "led directly to the termination of this activity in 1964" (CIA PDF p. 18).
2. **Approval and end, per the Church report.** The DCI approved MKULTRA on April 13, 1953, on a proposal from Richard Helms (Church Book I p. 390; hearing CIA PDF p. 100). The report speaks of the "ten-year life of the program" (Church p. 390) and says "according to the CIA" the (LSD testing) project was reduced each budget year until "complete termination in the late 1960s" (Church p. 392; CIA PDF p. 103). A CIA "material for the record" item says MKSEARCH was "the continuation of the MKULTRA program," funded FY 1966 to FY 1972 (CIA PDF p. 206).
3. **Subproject counts.** The recovered material: 149 MKULTRA subprojects "many of which appear to have some connection" with behavioral modification, drugs or covert drug administration; two boxes of miscellaneous papers; and 33 further subprojects with "nothing to do" with those topics (Turner, CIA PDF pp. 11 and 21). Turner sorts the 149 into 15 categories (CIA PDF pp. 12-14, 21-22); 6 involve tests on unwitting subjects (CIA PDF p. 12). The 1963 IG report is cited for 144 subprojects "related to the control of human behavior" initiated 1953-1963 (CIA PDF pp. 15, 23).
4. **Destruction of records.** Kennedy states the records were destroyed in January 1973 "at the instruction of then CIA Director Richard Helms" (CIA PDF p. 7). Turner's statement attributes it to "the then retiring Chief of the Office of Technical Service, with the authorization of the DCI" (CIA PDF p. 10). The Church report names the officer: records were destroyed in January 1973 by Technical Services Division personnel on the verbal orders of Dr. Sidney Gottlieb, Chief of TSD, who testified he was carrying out Helms's verbal order (Church p. 389; CIA PDF p. 99). A footnote adds that at least one assistant to Gottlieb protested the destruction (Church p. 404, fn. on CIA PDF p. 120).
5. **What survived.** Seven boxes of finance folders, filed by the Budget and Fiscal Section and sent to the Retired Records Center in 1970, so they escaped the 1973 destruction and the 1975 search (Turner, CIA PDF pp. 11, 19-20). Found in late March 1977 by an employee answering Freedom of Information Act requests (CIA PDF pp. 26-27); the committee was told in July (CIA PDF pp. 7, 26-28). Turner: mostly "approvals for advance of funds, vouchers, accountings"; no overall status reports or progress documents (CIA PDF pp. 12, 20). Roughly 5,000 pages estimated, possibly 8,000 (CIA PDF p. 27). Names recovered: 185 non-government researchers and assistants, 80 institutions, including 44 colleges or universities (CIA PDF pp. 15, 23). Kennedy gives "Eighty-six universities or institutions" (CIA PDF p. 8), which differs from Turner's 80.
6. **The records were thin before destruction too.** Turner quotes the 1963 IG report: "Present practice is to maintain no records of the planning and approval of test programs" (CIA PDF pp. 10, 19). The IG also wrote that a "substantial portion of the MKULTRA record appears to rest in the memories of the principal officers" and was likely lost on their departure (Church p. 404, fn. 77; CIA PDF p. 120). Turner adds that most of the CIA people involved in 1953-1964 had retired by 1975 (CIA PDF p. 19).
7. **Who knew, inside the CIA.** The Church report: few people "even within the agencies, knew of the programs" (Church p. 385; CIA PDF p. 93). Compartmentation "excluded the CIA's Medical Staff" from the principal research and testing program and may have led policymakers to get inconsistent answers (Church p. 386; CIA PDF p. 95). It finds the record unclear on who authorized specific projects; there is "no evidence" that DCI McCone was briefed on the details when he succeeded Dulles (Church p. 406; CIA PDF p. 122). Footnote 83 records conflict: an assistant to Gottlieb said the DCI and DDP got annual briefings; a 1963 IG memo said McCone had not been briefed in detail; McCone's executive officer said it was "perfectly apparent" neither McCone nor the DDCI was aware of the unwitting-testing project, "or if they had been briefed they had not understood it" (CIA PDF p. 122). In the Olson case the LSD experiment went ahead without notifying Wisner or Helms despite instructions (CIA PDF p. 123).
8. **Who knew, outside the CIA.** The Church report: "no evidence" the executive branch or Congress was ever informed (Church p. 385; CIA PDF p. 93). Turner (1977): no evidence of White House involvement or specific approval, "That does not say there was not" (CIA PDF pp. 25-26). IG official Brody: "no Presidential knowledge" to his knowledge (CIA PDF p. 26). On Congress, Turner: "no evidence one way or the other"; no records (CIA PDF p. 26). Turner: "Most of the people and institutions involved were not aware of CIA sponsorship" (CIA PDF p. 24); Kennedy: researchers and universities were used "without their knowledge" (CIA PDF p. 8).
9. **Oversight and controls.** In 1953 Helms proposed that six percent of TSD's R&D budget be spent "without the establishment of formal contractual relations"; Dulles approved April 13, 1953 (Church pp. 404-405; CIA PDF pp. 119-120). The CIA's Audit Branch head said the waivers produced "gross administrative failures" and kept the Office of General Counsel, the IG and the Audit Staff from supervising adequately (Church p. 386; CIA PDF p. 94). The IG discovered MKULTRA and the unwitting-LSD project in spring 1963 (Church p. 392; CIA PDF p. 103); a 1957 IG survey of TSD, per the Church report, was "an inspection which did not discover" that project (Church p. 391; CIA PDF p. 102).
10. **What the then-director said about results.** Turner (Sen. Huddleston's questions): the project "turned its character from a defensive to an offensive one"; it continued after it was understood foreign powers lacked such a drug (CIA PDF p. 58). Asked if it produced few useful results: "I think that is basically correct" (CIA PDF p. 59). Laubinger (Office of Technical Services): "I know of no drugs or anything like that developed under this program that ever reached operational use" (CIA PDF p. 59). Turner on testing methods: "totally abhorrent to me" to use a person as a guinea pig (CIA PDF p. 29). He also told the committee the CIA was running no drug experiments on humans in 1977 (CIA PDF p. 30).
11. **Counter-evidence on operational use.** The Church report says MKULTRA/MKDELTA materials "were used on a number of occasions" abroad, mainly as an aid to interrogation, also for harassment or disabling; because records were destroyed it cannot reconstruct the use (Church p. 391; CIA PDF p. 102). It cites the 1957 IG for six drugs developed for operational use, used in six operations on 33 subjects (Church pp. 391-392; CIA PDF p. 103).
12. **Money.** I found no program-wide total in either document. Figures that do appear: $375,000 CIA contribution to a hospital building fund through an intermediary, matched by federal funds (Turner, CIA PDF pp. 15-16, 23-24; Kennedy puts the matching funds at "more than $1 million," Turner says none of it paid for drug experiments, CIA PDF pp. 34-35); about $11,000 for three Fort Detrick-related subprojects, 1953-1960 (CIA PDF p. 14); Gittinger's guess of "about $150,000 a year" for one foundation, "don't hold me to that" (CIA PDF p. 86). The Church report says the CIA "had spent millions of dollars developing both materials and delivery systems" (Church p. 404, fn. 75; CIA PDF p. 120).
13. **Gottlieb in the hearing.** Turner's own statement and early testimony do not name Gottlieb (first hit in my text search: CIA PDF p. 62). Kennedy (a senator speaking, not a witness) calls Gottlieb "responsible for the whole program," says he had a "fuzzy memory" before the intelligence committee, and says "his name appears on just about every one of these documents" (CIA PDF pp. 62, 67). Turner on whether Gottlieb would know the safehouse activities: "it is unlikely" he would not; "I don't know Mr. Gottlieb"; Gottlieb "has left our employ" and had not been re-interviewed since the new records surfaced (CIA PDF p. 63). Gittinger (former CIA, testifying): asked if Gottlieb would know about the unwitting administration, "I believe so" (CIA PDF p. 84).

## XA. The 2008 financial crisis (Chapter X; also Chapters 1 and 4)

### 1. Primary document and how it was read

Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, *The Financial Crisis Inquiry Report: Final Report of the National Commission on the Causes of the Financial and Economic Crisis in the United States*, submitted January 2011. Downloaded from `https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-FCIC/pdf/GPO-FCIC.pdf` (5,595,383 bytes, SHA-256 `4ff588a66237fda69da2e08b162003716a08bce95be2e2fad0ee8c2d2825ae7c`), 663 PDF pages.
- **Limit:** the PDF's text layer **drops all digits** (for example "a  billion position"). I therefore rendered each cited page with `pdftoppm` and read the page image to get numbers and printed page numbers. Words came from `pdftotext`.
- **Page numbers.** Printed page numbers below are those printed on the page (roman numerals for the front-matter "Conclusions"; arabic for the body). For the body, printed page = PDF page minus 29, which I confirmed by reading the printed page number on pages 55, 147, 149, 243, 329, 339 and 386 and on pages 221 and 511 (header crops).
- **Read:** the whole "Conclusions of the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission" (pp. xvi-xxviii, text) and, in the body, pp. 55, 146-150, 243, 329, 339, 386 (text plus page images for 243, 147, 329 and header crops for the rest). I searched the full text for "correlation," "unaware," "clear grasp," "whole picture," "did not know," "Reserve Primary." I did **not** read the other ~620 pages, the dissents (the PDF contains them: the page header at PDF p. 540 reads "Peter J. Wallison," printed p. 511), or the footnotes.

### 2. Receipts (printed page numbers)

1. **p. xvii.** The Commission concludes the crisis was avoidable, "the result of human action and inaction, not of Mother Nature or computer models gone haywire." (The sentence continues: "ignored warnings and failed to question, understand, and manage evolving risks".)
2. **p. xix.** "Financial institutions and credit rating agencies embraced mathematical models as reliable predictors of risks, replacing judgment in too many instances." Then: "Too often, risk management became risk justification."
3. **p. xix.** Governance examples: the Citigroup CEO said a $40 billion position in highly rated mortgage securities "would not in any way have excited my attention"; AIG senior management's "ignorance of the terms and risks" of a $79 billion derivatives exposure; Merrill Lynch's top management's "costly surprise" at holding $55 billion in "super-senior" securities.
4. **p. xxi.** Regulators "did not have a clear grasp of the financial system they were charged with overseeing"; "They thought risk had been diversified when, in fact, it had been concentrated"; they "lacked a full understanding of the risks and interconnections." And: a month before Lehman's collapse the New York Fed "was still seeking information" on exposures from Lehman's "more than 900,000 derivatives contracts."
5. **p. xxv.** Moody's examined as a case study: from 2000 to 2007 it rated nearly 45,000 mortgage-related securities triple-A; in 2006 alone 30 every working day; 83% of those rated triple-A that year were later downgraded. The Commission lists "the flawed computer models" among the forces behind Moody's breakdowns, alongside payer pressure, market share and lack of resources.
6. **pp. 146-147 (Chapter "The CDO Machine," section "Moody's: 'Achieved Through Some Alchemy'").** Moody's had to estimate the correlation of defaults; the report's analogy contrasts coin flips with "a loaf of sliced bread." Moody's relied almost entirely on the ratings already assigned to the mortgage securities and did not "look through" to the underlying mortgages. Gary Witt, a former Moody's managing director, said Moody's lacked a good correlation model, so "they made them up" (p. 147). He developed a methodology in mid-2004 that was not applied to CDO ratings for a year; his early-2005 "look through" proposal was approved but he could not buy the software because of contractual disagreements; a June 2005 update used 20 years of benign data (p. 147).
7. **p. 149.** "In retrospect, it is clear that the agencies' CDO models made two key mistakes": they assumed diversification across many mortgage-backed securities "when in fact these securities weren't that different," and rated CDOs on ratings they had themselves assigned. In late 2008 (read from the page image), after the crisis, Moody's replaced its key CDO assumptions with an asset correlation assumption two to three times higher than before (p. 149).
8. **p. 55.** Fed Chairman Bernanke told the FCIC that the "Fed-Lite" structure of 1999 "made it difficult for any single regulator to reliably see the whole picture of activities and risks of large, complex banking institutions."
9. **p. 243.** At AIG Financial Products, which had written $79 billion in credit default swap protection, the CEO, CFO, chief risk officer, chief credit officer and a division CFO "told FCIC investigators that they did not even know about" the collateral-call terms until the calls began in July 2007; the Office of Thrift Supervision "didn't know either." The salesman and the division CEO did know the terms, "And the counterparties knew, of course."
10. **p. 329.** Before Lehman's failure, regulators "did not know nearly enough about over-the-counter derivatives activities" at Lehman; investment banks disclosed totals but not contract terms or counterparties, so "there was no way to know who would be owed how much and when." New York Fed officials were "very reluctant" to request Lehman's master agreements because it would send a "huge negative signal."
11. **p. 339.** After Lehman filed, Bernanke described the chain: Lehman's commercial paper held by the Reserve Primary Fund, a run on money market funds, a spike in the commercial paper market, trouble for General Motors.
12. **p. 386 (Commission conclusions, Chapter 20).** "Many firms did not know their own exposures." Derivatives created "interconnections among a vast web of financial institutions through counterparty credit risk."

### 3. Does it support the book, and the slate's angle?

**Book, chapter 1 (the 2008 section):** banks relied on risk models for individual assets, believing diversification protected them; models "largely failed to account for the systemic correlation"; "everyone was using similar models"; Lehman's failure "triggered a cascade." **Chapter 4:** rating agencies "assigned AAA ratings to these toxic assets"; failure of one institution, "e.g., Lehman," a domino effect; derivatives opacity "made it impossible to assess who held what risk." **Chapter X Thread 2:** the 2008 system "optimized returns without coordinating for systemic correlation."

**For the slate's angle ("where the knowing sat"):** **partly supported.** Several passages show knowing in one place and not another (AIG's salesman and CEO knew the terms, top executives and the OTS did not, receipt 9; regulators lacked the whole picture, 4, 8, 10; firms did not know their own exposures, 12). So "nobody held the knowing" is too strong; "the knowing sat with a few people and not with the people who decided" is closer to what I read.

**Too broad for a 6-minute episode? Yes.** The report is 600+ pages and its Conclusions alone list nine separate findings (regulation, governance and risk management, leverage and opacity, government preparedness, ethics, mortgage securitization, OTC derivatives, rating agencies, and "avoidable"), plus discussion of the GSEs, liquidity and housing policy (pp. xvii-xxviii). The narrower episodes the pages I read support best:
- **Moody's and the correlation assumption** (pp. 146-150): matches the book's "models failed to account for correlation" claim most directly, and has a named insider (Witt) whose fix was delayed. Needs the rest of that chapter section (not read) before scripting.
- **AIG Financial Products and the collateral-call terms** (p. 243 and the AIG chapters): the cleanest "who held the knowing" story I found, with the people who knew and did not know named in one page. I read only p. 243 and the conclusions; the other AIG pages would need to be read first.
- Not recommended: "2008 in general."

### 4. Differences from the book's own telling

- The book's Chapter 4 line that 2008 "wasn't irrational behavior OR failed models" is compatible with the FCIC's first conclusion; the Chapter 1 sentence ("these models largely failed") is more model-centred than the report's own conclusion (receipt 1, p. xvii).
- The book says nothing of the Commission's finding that the Federal Reserve failed to set mortgage-lending standards, or that regulators "chose not to use" power they had (p. xviii).
- The book's "sophisticated risk models for individual assets and portfolios" (Chapter 1) is a bank-side claim I did not find in the pages I read; the model passages I read concern rating agencies.

### 5. New status for slot XA

**READ** for the Conclusions chapter and the eight body pages listed (pp. 55, 146-150, 243, 329, 339, 386); **the remaining ~620 pages and the dissents were not read.** Reason: enough to cite the "who held the whole picture" passages; not enough to script a full-crisis episode. Recommend narrowing to a single institution (AIG Financial Products) or a single decision/process (Moody's CDO correlation) and reading that section in full first.

## XB. Moffatt v. Air Canada (Chapter X; not in the book)

### 1. Primary document and how it was read

*Moffatt v. Air Canada*, **2024 BCCRT 149**, British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal, file SC-2023-005609, small claims, **decision issued February 14, 2024**; Tribunal Member Christopher C. Rivers. URL: `https://decisions.civilresolutionbc.ca/crt/crtd/en/525448/1/document.do`. Downloaded with curl (a plain curl with a "Mozilla" user agent and no Accept header got HTTP 403; adding an Accept header that allows PDF returned the PDF), 191,298 bytes, SHA-256 `4dd97ce46d0d3f858488dd5ddfac85ec6fb993fcf7ba85567bd2875548f7e394`, 9 pages, text extracted with `pdftotext -layout`. **I read the entire decision.** Locators are paragraph numbers (¶) and the PDF's printed page numbers.

### 2. Receipts

1. **The question (¶11, p. 3).** "Did Air Canada negligently misrepresent the procedure for claiming bereavement fares, and if so, what is the remedy?" Mr. Moffatt is self-represented; Air Canada is represented by an employee (¶5). The decision uses the pronoun "they" for Moffatt.
2. **Facts (¶¶13, 18).** Moffatt's grandmother died in Ontario on **November 11, 2022**. That day Moffatt booked a one-way Vancouver-Toronto flight (departing Nov 12) for **$794.98**; on **November 16** booked Toronto-Vancouver (departing Nov 18) for **$845.38**, "relying on the same information."
3. **What the chatbot said (¶15, p. 4).** The screenshot says Air Canada offers reduced bereavement fares, and tells a traveller who has "already travelled" to submit the ticket for a reduced rate "within 90 days of the date your ticket was issued" using the Ticket Refund Application form.
4. **What the policy page said (¶¶16-17).** The words "bereavement fares" in the chatbot reply were a hyperlink to a page titled "Bereavement travel." That page says the policy does not apply to requests made after travel has been completed. The tribunal says it addresses "the inconsistency between Air Canada's chatbot and webpage" later (¶17).
5. **What the customer did (¶¶19-23).** A phone call to an Air Canada representative on Nov 11: told the fare would be approximately $380 each way; no evidence the representative said anything about retroactive applications (¶19). First application **November 17, 2022**; correspondence through December 2022 to February 2023 (¶20). On **February 8** an Air Canada representative admitted the chatbot had given "misleading words," pointed to the link, and said the issue was noted so the chatbot could be updated (¶22).
6. **The nature of the chatbot (¶14).** The tribunal notes "Air Canada did not provide any information about the nature of its chatbot," defines a chatbot generally, and records that the parties "implicitly agree" Moffatt was not chatting with an employee. **The decision does not say the chatbot used generative AI, who built it, or how it produced the answer.**
7. **Air Canada's arguments (¶¶4, 27, 31, 35, 41).** Moffatt did not follow the proper procedure and could not claim retroactively (¶4). Air Canada "cannot be held liable for the information provided by the chatbot" (¶4), which the tribunal characterises as in effect suggesting the chatbot "is a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions" and calls "a remarkable submission" (¶27). It also relied on its Domestic Tariff (¶¶4, 31), argued the correct information was on another part of the site (¶28), and argued a $200 goodwill coupon should be set off (¶41).
8. **Reasoning on responsibility (¶¶27-28).** The chatbot "is still just a part of Air Canada's website"; it "should be obvious" Air Canada is responsible for all information on its website, and "It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot." Air Canada did not take reasonable care to ensure the chatbot was accurate; it did not explain why the "Bereavement travel" page was "inherently more trustworthy" than the chatbot, or why customers should have to double-check one part of its site against another (¶28).
9. **Reliance and tariff (¶¶29-31).** Reliance was reasonable; Moffatt would not have flown last-minute had they known they would pay the full fare (¶¶29-30). Air Canada gave only submissions about what the tariff says, not the tariff; the tribunal says a party that wants a contractual defence must provide the contract, so no contractual defence was proven (¶31). The tribunal finds negligent misrepresentation made out (¶32); duty of care from the commercial relationship (¶26).
10. **Damages and award (¶¶33-45, p. 7-9).** Moffatt claimed $880.36 (¶34); the tribunal says the actual difference is $870.36, the most that could be owed (¶34). It drew an adverse inference against Air Canada for not producing evidence of a different bereavement fare and took $380 as the market value (¶¶35-36). Paid $1,630.36; should have paid **$979.48** (flight 1 $474.23 per ¶38, flight 2 $505.25 per ¶39) so damages **$650.88** (¶40). No set-off for the $200 coupon: Moffatt says it was not accepted and Air Canada gave no evidence otherwise (¶41). Pre-judgment interest **$36.14** from Nov 17, 2022 (¶42); CRT fees **$125** (¶43). **Order (¶44): $812.02 within 14 days** = $650.88 + $36.14 + $125. Remaining claims dismissed (¶46).

### 3. Does it support the slate's description and angle?

**Slate (XB):** the tribunal "rejected Air Canada's argument that it was not liable for its chatbot's information" - **supported** (receipts 7-8).
**Fit with the channel's premise ("failure because nobody held the knowing"): partly.** What the decision documents is a mismatch between two parts of one company's website that Air Canada did not reconcile (receipts 3, 4, 8) and a company that treated the bot as separate from itself (7). It does not document how the mismatch arose, who owned the chatbot's content, or whether anyone at Air Canada knew of the discrepancy before February 2023 (it records only the Feb 8 admission and "noted the issue," receipt 5). So the case fits "accountability sat nowhere until the tribunal put it on the company" better than "nobody held the knowing"; the second half would be inference. The case is not about a failure at scale (an $812.02 order).

### 4. Differences from the book

The book (chapters 1-9, X, as grepped) does not mention this case; the slate also lists it as "No." No differences to report.

### 2. Receipts

1. **Board decision and the stated reason (8-K Item 2.05).** On Nov 2, 2021 the Board "made the determination to wind down Zillow Offers operations," "in light of home pricing unpredictability, capacity constraints and other operational challenges" that were "exacerbated by an unprecedented housing market, a global pandemic and a difficult labor and supply chain environment." Same wording in the 10-Q, Note 20 and MD&A (10-Q, Item 2).
2. **Write-down (8-K Item 2.05; 10-Q Note 5).** The company recorded a **$304.4 million write-down of inventory** associated with Zillow Offers "as a result of purchasing homes during the third quarter at higher prices than Zillow Group's current estimates of future selling prices after selling costs." The 10-Q (Note 5) adds that before Q3 2021 it "did not record any material write-downs to inventory" and the write-down was recorded in cost of revenue. The press release rounds to "approximately $304 million" in the Homes segment.
3. **Further charges (8-K Item 2.05).** Expected: about **$240 million to $265 million** on homes under contract at September 30 that "may be canceled without significant penalty," in Q4 2021 cost of revenue; and about **$175 million to $230 million** of wind-down charges, of which an estimated $110-150 million employee termination, $15-20 million long-lived assets, $5-10 million contract termination, and about $40-50 million financing-facility wind-down (interest expense). Workforce reduction about **25%**. All "subject to change."
4. **The forecasting statement.** In the press release the CEO is quoted: "unpredictability in forecasting home prices far exceeds what we anticipated" (Exhibit 99.1, p. 1). In the shareholder letter (p. 2): the aim was to be "a market maker, not a market risk taker," "underpinned by the need to forecast the price of homes accurately three to six months into the future," with public unit-economics guardrails of "+/- 200 basis points of breakeven."
5. **What the letter says went wrong (Ex. 99.3, p. 2).** The company has been "unable to accurately forecast future home prices at different times in both directions by much more than we modeled as possible"; unit economics swung "approximately 1,200 basis points from Q2 to an expected -500 to -700 basis points in Q4 2021." Further volatility "would require too much equity capital."
6. **Q3 operating facts (Ex. 99.3, pp. 6, 8).** "Higher conversion rates than previously observed" meant purchases of **9,680 homes** in Q3 against **3,032 sold**; the company says it was "unintentionally purchasing homes at higher prices than our current estimates of future selling prices." Q3 ending inventory 9,790 homes, 8,172 under contract. Zillow Offers gross profit was a loss of $245 million; average gross profit per home sold a loss of $80,771; Homes segment loss before income taxes $422 million.
7. **Other reasons the letter gives (pp. 2-3).** Capacity and demand-planning problems "exacerbated by a difficult labor and supply chain environment" and the earlier suspension of new contracts (announced Oct 18, 2021; 10-Q MD&A); too little return on equity; and the company had "been able to convert only about 10%" of serious sellers who asked for an offer, disappointing the other roughly 90%.
8. **The Zestimate and the model.** I searched every document for "Zestimate": **zero hits** in the 8-K, the press release and the 10-Q. In the shareholder letter it appears **only on p. 3**, in the passage on why the company sees "better, broader, less risky" ways to serve movers: "given... the popularity of the Zestimate," and later that "our ability to continually improve the Zestimate" is "fundamental and foundational" to the audience advantage. That text is about the consumer-facing product as a top-of-funnel asset; it does not say the Zestimate drove, or did not drive, Zillow Offers pricing. **No document names a pricing, valuation or forecasting model or algorithm for Zillow Offers.** The word "modeled" appears once (receipt 5) and refers to the company's planning assumptions.
9. **Accounting estimates.** The 10-Q lists "the net realizable value of inventory" among estimates and warns of "unanticipated changes to management's estimates" (10-Q risk factor, "Our financial projections..."); Note 5 describes the write-down as the carrying cost exceeding net realizable value based on "current estimates of future selling prices." These are management's estimates; the filing does not say how they are produced.

### 3. Does it support a claim that a pricing model caused the losses?

**No. The filings support only the company's own stated explanation:** it bought homes at prices above its later estimates of resale prices (receipt 2), it could not forecast prices as accurately as it had planned (4, 5), and conversion ran higher than expected while labour and supply constraints bound (6, 7). They do not say a model was wrong, name a model, say whether an algorithm or a person set offer prices, or connect the Zestimate to the losses (8). Any such claim would need a source not read here. Equally, the filings do not exclude one.
**Slate check:** "$304.4 million inventory write-down" - **confirmed**; "does not mention the Zestimate in the excerpts I saw" - **confirmed for the whole of the 8-K, the press release and the 10-Q; the shareholder letter mentions it, as brand and funnel, not as a pricing tool.**
