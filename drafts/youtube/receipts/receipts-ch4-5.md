## 4A. Diabetic-retinopathy screening in Thai clinics (Google)

**Slot:** Chapter 4, A ("The Clinic With the Bad Lighting"). Old status LOCATED.

### 1. Primary document and how it was read

- Emma Beede, Elizabeth Baylor, Fred Hersch, Anna Iurchenko, Lauren Wilcox, Paisan Ruamviboonsuk, Laura M. Vardoulakis. *A Human-Centered Evaluation of a Deep Learning System Deployed in Clinics for the Detection of Diabetic Retinopathy.* CHI '20, Honolulu, April 25-30, 2020, paper 589, pp. 1-12. doi:10.1145/3313831.3376718. Authors are at Google Health, with one at Rajavithi Hospital, Bangkok.
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376718 (ACM page https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3313831.3376718; Google Research page https://research.google/pubs/pub48768/).
- **How obtained:** `curl` to dl.acm.org returned HTTP 403, so I used an Internet Archive capture of the ACM PDF URL (`web.archive.org/web/2021/https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3313831.3376718`, 6.5 MB PDF, 12 pages, `pdfinfo` title matches). The PDF's own metadata shows creation Jan 2020 and modification Feb 2020, so it is the camera-ready file, but I did not compare it to the ACM-hosted version.
- **What I read:** all 12 pages, text extracted with `pdftotext` and read through in order (abstract, intro, related work, DR screening in Thailand, context of use, protocol, data collection, participants, findings, discussion, limitations, conclusion). I also searched the whole text for shutdown-type words (see receipt 10). Figures 1-5 are images; I did not view them.
- Also read, only as a pointer (as the brief said): arXiv 2012.01165, "AI in the 'Real World'" (6 pages, Nov 2020), section 3 only. It is a secondary paper that summarises Beede et al.

### 2. Receipts

1. **Study design.** Human-centred observational fieldwork at **11 clinics** in two provinces, 5 in Pathum Thani and 6 in Chiang Mai, in **November 2018, April 2019 and August 2019**. (Beede et al., "Human-Centered Clinical Field Study" section, p. 4; Table 1 caption, p. 5.)
2. **Fieldwork volume.** Pre-deployment: 26 hours observing at 11 clinics (over 100 patients observed) and 15 hours interviewing 13 nurses at 8 clinics. Post-deployment: 12 hours observing about 50 patients and 7 hours of interviews with five nurses and one camera technician. Analysis also used system logs from 1,838 image uploads. (Data Collection and Data Analysis, p. 5.)
3. **What was "deployed".** The system was deployed at **three** clinics in Pathum Thani (Klong Luang, Nongsue, Lamlukka) as part of a prospective study of 7,600 patients run with Rajavithi Hospital under the Ministry of Public Health. Those three sites screened patients from December 2018 to May 2019, when their eye-screening season ended, "to be resumed in October 2019". The paper's fieldwork ran in parallel with that study. (Context of use, p. 3.) Table 1's caption (p. 5) says clinics 2, 4 and 5 were visited post-deployment in April 2019; clinics 9-11 and 6-8 were pre-deployment only.
4. **Image-quality threshold (the main problem).** The system was designed to assess only the highest-quality images "for patient safety reasons"; a bit of blur or a dark area gets the image rejected as ungradable. Of **1,838 images in the first six months of usage, 393 (21%) did not meet the standard**. The authors say ungradability "had the largest impact" of the factors they found. (Gradability, p. 8; Discussion, p. 9.)
5. **Lighting.** Only two of the 11 clinics had a screening room that could be darkened; elsewhere screening took place in nurses' offices or shared rooms, and lights were rarely turned off. Causes of dark images named: room light, small pupils from cataract, a camera needing repair, and no dilation drops in Pathum Thani. (Clinic Screening Conditions, p. 6; Gradability, p. 8.) The authors' own fixes (darken room, cloth hood, wait 60 seconds between eyes) were judged "difficult to implement in practice" because of shared rooms and queues of up to 150 patients. (p. 8.)
6. **Connectivity.** Images are uploaded to the cloud for grading. Some uploads took **60-90 seconds**; in one clinic the internet was out for two hours and the number of patients screened fell from 200 to 100. One nurse said that in the first two hours they could screen only 10 patients. (Internet speed and connectivity, p. 8.)
7. **What nurses did.** (a) They took more photos to get a gradable one, but typically tried twice and never a third time because of the flash discomfort, spending 2-4 minutes per patient (p. 8). (b) They stitched a "composite" from two partial photos by eye, which the system cannot do because it needs one high-quality image per eye (p. 8). (c) For ungradable images they **worked around the study protocol**: if the patient had no DR history and well-controlled blood sugar, a nurse sometimes sent the patient home without referral (pp. 8-9, nurses P6 and P8). (d) At clinics 4 and 5 nurses dissuaded patients from joining the study for fear of the travel burden (p. 7).
8. **Referral.** Under the protocol an ungradable image meant referral to Pathum Thani Hospital, described as an hour's drive away. The paper says 50% of patients at clinic 4 opted out of the study; nurse P6 put it at 40-50% who decline because they think they will have to go to hospital. Nurses were "hesitant to refer" on ungradability alone. (Consenting patients, p. 7; Workarounds, p. 9.)
9. **Protocol change made in response.** Because of this research, "the study protocol now delays the patient referral for an ungradable image until" the ophthalmologist overreader has decided; the authors expect this to lower referral rates. (Discussion, p. 9.) The authors also say many of the environmental problems could be reduced by lighting changes and camera repairs but might be costly or infeasible in low-resource settings (p. 9).
10. **What the paper says about shutdowns: nothing.** I searched the full extracted text for "shut", "discontinu", "abandon", "stopp", "withdr", "terminat", "halt", "suspend", "decommission" and "pull"; the only hits were unrelated ("progression of DR can be stopped", "pull patients aside"). The paper does not report any clinic or hospital stopping use, and it does not report what happened to the system after the fieldwork. Its last statements on the future are that the authors "have begun to hold participatory design workshops" with nurses and specialists "at future deployment sites" (Limitations and Future Work, p. 10) and that the work was done "prior to widespread deployment" (Conclusion, p. 10). It describes the system as being used in a prospective study, not as having been rolled out to "hospitals".
11. **Accuracy figures in the paper.** The paper cites ">90% sensitivity and specificity" for referable DR, and a retrospective comparison with human graders showing a 23% cut in false negatives at the cost of a 2% rise in false positives (Introduction, p. 2). There is no "95%".
12. **Secondary paper.** arXiv 2012.01165 §3 retells the study and calls the outcome "failures of this AI system once deployed", but it also does not say any site shut the system down. It is not primary; it is listed only because a reader may meet it first.

### 3. Does the source support the slate's description and angle?

- **Slate description** ("Google's own observational study across 11 clinics in Thailand... tensions between image-quality thresholds and conditions in clinics (lighting, connectivity, workflow)"): **supported** (receipts 1, 4-6).
- **Angle** ("where the knowing sat"): **supported, with a caution.** The paper's own finding is that nurses' local knowledge (when a blurry image is still readable; whom a hospital trip would burden; the patient's blood-sugar history) was not in the system's threshold or the study protocol, and nurses substituted their own judgement (receipts 4, 7, 8). The caution: the paper does not frame it as "nobody held the knowing"; it frames it as socio-environmental factors and a protocol that was iterated (receipt 9). It is also a *study with ongoing iteration*, not a documented failure. An episode should say so and not call it "the system failed".
- **Fit to the channel premise** (a failure because nobody held the knowing): **partial.** There is friction, workarounds and a protocol revision, but no documented collapse. Keep only if the episode is honest that the source describes problems found during a study.

### 4. Differences from the book's telling

Book text (ch. 4, "Example: Diabetic Retinopathy Detection", then "What specifically went wrong"): "95% accuracy"; "Hospitals deployed it. Many shut it down within months."; doctors "didn't trust it"; false positives "scared patients"; "who pays for follow-up?"; reports "in a separate format" that doctors had to transcribe into EHR systems. The book does not name Thailand, Google or the paper.

- "Hospitals deployed it. Many shut it down within months": **not in the paper** (receipt 10). The paper reports a three-clinic deployment inside a prospective study.
- "95% accuracy": paper says ">90% sensitivity and specificity" (receipt 11). Not the same figure.
- "Doctors didn't trust it": the users in the paper are **nurses** and camera technicians. Nurses questioned the system when it rejected images they could read (receipt 4, quotes from P6 and P3, p. 8). The paper does not report ophthalmologists distrusting it.
- "False positives scared patients": the paper reports nurses *anticipating* worry about false positives (Potential challenges, p. 7). The harm it actually observed was travel burden from referral.
- "Who pays for follow-up" and "EHR integration/separate report format": I searched the extracted text for "electronic", "EHR", "health record", "who pays", "reimburs", "insurance" and "economics" and found none. Cost appears only as patient cost of missing work or travel (pp. 7, 9).
- The slate's section 4 conflict ("shutdown claim was not found") is **confirmed** now that the paper was read.

### 5. New status

**READ.** Reason: I read the whole paper (12 pp.) from an Internet Archive copy of the ACM PDF. The book's shutdown claim is not in it. The paper's own account (a three-clinic prospective study with gradability, connectivity and referral problems and a protocol change) is the one an episode can rely on. Not done: figures not viewed; the later published results of the prospective study (a web-search result pointed to a related Google Research publication on real-time DR screening in a multisite national programme) were not read, so what happened to the deployment after 2019 is **unknown** from my reading.

## 4C. Big Dig ceiling collapse, Boston, July 10, 2006

**Slot:** Chapter 4, C ("The Ceiling"). Old status LOCATED.

### 1. Primary document and how it was read

- National Transportation Safety Board. *Ceiling Collapse in the Interstate 90 Connector Tunnel, Boston, Massachusetts, July 10, 2006.* Highway Accident Report NTSB/HAR-07/02 (PB2007-916203), adopted July 10, 2007. 132 PDF pages.
- URL: https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/HAR0702.pdf (linked from https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/Pages/HWY06MH024.aspx). Downloaded with `curl` (3.6 MB), text extracted with `pdftotext`.
- **What I read:** abstract and Executive Summary (pp. viii-ix), the table of "Selected Entities Involved" (p. vii), the accident synopsis (p. 1), the CA/T overview (p. 15), the epoxy testing and anchor-approval passages (pp. 31-34), the "Preaccident Anchor Failures" account (pp. 42-49), Analysis sections "Response to Preaccident Anchor Failures" (pp. 95-98), the Powers product-information passage (pp. 93-95), "Lack of Awareness of the Potential for Adhesive Anchor Creep" (pp. 101-102), all 20 Findings and the Probable Cause (pp. 105-108), and part of the tunnel-inspection discussion (pp. 76-78). I did **not** read the engineering test chapters (anchor testing, laboratory results, pp. 50-73), the other analysis sections (installation procedures, standards, tunnel finishes), the recommendations or the appendices.

### 2. Receipts

1. **Event and casualties.** About 11:01 p.m. on Monday, July 10, 2006, a section of the suspended concrete ceiling in the eastbound I-90 connector tunnel (D Street portal) fell onto a 1991 Buick; about **26 tons** of concrete and hardware fell. The driver's wife, in the right-front seat, was fatally injured; the driver escaped with minor injuries. (Executive Summary p. viii; Accident Synopsis p. 1.) The report gives their ages (46 and 38); I did not see names in the passages I read.
2. **Probable cause, as written.** "The use of an epoxy anchor adhesive with poor creep resistance, that is, an epoxy formulation that was not capable of sustaining long-term loads"; over time the epoxy deformed and fractured until anchors pulled free. (Executive Summary p. viii; Probable Cause p. 107.)
3. **Who failed at what, as the Board words it.** Gannett Fleming and Bechtel/Parsons Brinckerhoff failed "to identify potential creep in the anchor adhesive as a critical long-term failure mode"; this also reflected "a general lack of understanding and knowledge in the construction community"; Powers Fasteners failed to give the project complete, accurate information about its Fast Set epoxy; Powers also failed to find that the 1999 displacement was creep. Modern Continental and B/PB failed to keep monitoring anchors after 1999; the Massachusetts Turnpike Authority failed to start timely tunnel inspections. (pp. viii, 107-108.)
4. **Roles of the named organizations.** FHWA funded and approved; MassHighway awarded contracts; the Massachusetts Turnpike Authority (MTA) owned and operated the completed project; B/PB was management consultant, the MTA's representative and construction manager, and secondary reviewer of the anchor submittal; Gannett Fleming designed the tunnel finishes and was primary reviewer of the anchor submittal; Modern Continental was the contractor that selected and installed the anchors; Conam did the proof tests; Newman Renner Colony supplied the anchor system; Powers Fasteners supplied the epoxy; Sika Corporation formulated it. (Table "Selected Entities Involved", p. vii.)
5. **What Powers knew, and when.** The report says the Fast Set epoxy had been creep-tested in 1995-96 and **failed**; anchors tested at 110 degrees F pulled out in under two days; "No evidence was found" that the Fast Set formulation "had ever passed a creep test at any temperature". An Aug 15, 1996 letter from Powers to Sika referred to the failed creep test and asked Sika to see whether the formulation could be changed; it was not reformulated. (Factual Information, pp. 32-33.) The Board says that before the epoxy went to the D Street portal Powers "had conclusive evidence" its Fast Set epoxy was susceptible to creep (p. 94), and that its literature did not distinguish Fast Set from Standard Set; Powers revised its literature to say Fast Set was for short-term loads only in **May 2007**, after the accident (p. 94). The Standard Set version had passed a 120-day creep test in 1996-97 (p. 33).
6. **What the project's designers and builders knew.** The report found "no evidence" that Modern Continental, Gannett Fleming or B/PB knew the adhesive was susceptible to creep (p. 95), and says "No one involved with the CA/T project appeared to be aware" that such an epoxy could deform under sustained load (p. 101). Finding 8: Modern Continental was not aware. Finding 10: Gannett Fleming approved the anchors without identifying which formulation was used, even though it had information that one version "should be used for short-term loading only". (Findings 8 and 10, pp. 105-106.)
7. **The 1999 anchor displacement.** Sept 9, 1999: a Modern Continental worker noticed hanger plates in the HOV tunnel ceiling mock-up displaced about 1/16 inch; by early October the worst was 1/2 inch (later 9/16). Oct 7, 1999: Modern Continental notified B/PB. Oct 12: B/PB suggested over-stress or poor installation. Powers visited on Oct 13 and 21 and in a letter of Oct 29, 1999 said adhesive problems "typically reveal themselves immediately" and pointed to over-torque. (pp. 42-45.) No evidence Powers acted further (p. 45; p. 97).
8. **What was done.** The anchors that moved were removed and replaced; all previously installed HOV-area anchors were retested to a higher proof load (6,350 lb instead of 3,250 lb); 187 anchors were retested with **19 failures**; B/PB closed its Deficiency Report 001 on Jan 26, 2001. (pp. 46-49.) In December 1999 a B/PB structural engineer wrote internally that an explanation of why the anchors failed was "glaringly absent" from the deficiency report (p. 47). B/PB rejected the first fix proposal on Dec 27, 1999 (pp. 47-48).
9. **The warning the fix missed.** Dec 17, 2001: a Modern Continental inspector reported more anchors pulling out in another part of the tunnel, some of which had passed the higher 6,350-lb test a few months earlier; the report's reason was "unknown". B/PB's field engineers also logged anchor "slippage" in 2001 and 2002 without deficiency reports. (p. 49; p. 96.) The Board concluded that the fix "did nothing to prevent future displacement" and that B/PB and Modern Continental treated later failures as "isolated instances" rather than systemically (pp. 96-97).
10. **Monitoring, per the Board.** Finding 13: after unexplained displacement in 1999 and 2001, B/PB and Modern Continental "should have instituted a program to monitor anchor performance"; had they done so they "likely would have found that anchor creep was occurring". Finding 15: MTA inspections of the area above the ceilings between Nov 2003 and July 2006 would likely have caught it. The MTA officials said an inspection manual published in Nov 2003 was not used before the collapse. (pp. 97, 106, 76-77.)
11. **The Board's own statement of the knowledge problem.** "A striking lack of knowledge among the designers, contractors, managers, and overseers" about polymer adhesives; "a general lack of knowledge and understanding" in the field (Analysis, pp. 101-102; Finding 18, p. 106). It also notes short-term proof tests could not show long-term performance (Finding 12).
12. **Project cost in the report.** The report says the project was originally scheduled for 1998 at **$2.6 billion** and was completed in 2006 at "a final project cost in excess of $14 billion"; it calls it a "20-year effort". (CA/T overview, p. 15.) It does not give $22 billion.

### 3. Does the source support the slate's description and a "knowledge existed but did not reach the decision" angle?

- **Slate description** (probable cause is an epoxy adhesive with poor creep resistance; NTSB faulted principals for not recognising creep as a critical failure mode and for not monitoring anchors after the 1999 displacement; about 26 tons; one death): **supported** (receipts 1-3, 7-10).
- **Angle "knowledge existed but did not reach the decision": partly supported.** Supported at one company: Powers had failed creep test results from 1995-96 and a 1996 letter about them, and the Board says its product literature and its 1999 response did not carry that knowledge to the project (receipts 5, 7). Not supported at the project level: the Board says nobody on the project appeared to know epoxies could creep, and attributes it to a general knowledge gap in the construction community (receipts 6, 11). So a more accurate framing is two linked failures: (a) one supplier held the information and did not pass it on or notice it in 1999; (b) the owners, designers and builders did not hold the concept at all, and when evidence arrived in 1999 and 2001 they treated it as an installation problem and stopped looking. Any script should keep these separate and not claim that "the project knew".
- **Fit to the channel premise:** **good.** The report's text is unusually direct about knowledge, monitoring and who was responsible for what. Risk: the report assigns shares of fault, with legal consequences I did not look into (settlements, prosecutions, civil suits). Those are outside the NTSB report and unchecked.

### 4. Differences from the book's telling

The book names the Big Dig only in one sentence: "Boston's Big Dig: $22 billion and fifteen years solving 1960s traffic problems in a 2007 city—government optimizing without market or community coordination." (ch. 4.) Differences:
- **Cost:** the NTSB report gives "in excess of $14 billion" final project cost and a "20-year effort... more than 14 years in the major construction phase" (receipt 12). The $22 billion and "fifteen years" figures are not in this report; they might come from other sources (e.g. including financing costs) which I did not check.
- The book does not mention the collapse, the epoxy or the 1999 warning at all. The NTSB report does not frame the collapse as "government optimizing without market or community coordination"; it names contractors, consultants, a supplier and the owner agency (receipt 4). The slate was right that the episode would be about the collapse, not the book's use.

## 5A. Mata v. Avianca, Inc. (S.D.N.Y. 22-cv-1461)

**Slot:** Chapter 5, A ("The Citations That Did Not Exist"). Old status LOCATED.

### 1. Primary documents and how they were read

- **Order to Show Cause**, May 4, 2023, ECF 31 (3 pp.), Judge P. Kevin Castel. Obtained as a PDF from CourtListener's RECAP archive: `storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368.31.0.pdf`. **Read in full.**
- **Opinion and Order on Sanctions**, June 22, 2023, ECF 54 (43 pp.; the opinion proper is pp. 1-34, then appendices of the fake "Varghese" opinion and ChatGPT screenshots). Same archive, `...575368.54.0.pdf`. **Read pp. 1-34 in full** (Findings of Fact 1-51, Conclusions of Law 1-34, Conclusion). The appendices (pp. 35-43) are images and I did not read them.
- I did **not** use the courts.ri.gov or docketalarm copies named in the brief; these RECAP copies carry the court's ECF stamps ("Case 1:22-cv-01461-PKC Document 54 Filed 06/22/23"). I did not verify the reporter citation (678 F. Supp. 3d 443) because the slip opinion does not carry it.
- Locators: "FoF" = Finding of Fact, "CoL" = Conclusion of Law, "p." = the opinion's own page number at the page foot.

### 2. Receipts

1. **Sequence of filings and orders (from FoF).** Complaint in state court on or about Feb 2, 2022; removed Feb 22, 2022 (FoF 1, p. 3). Avianca moved to dismiss as time-barred on Jan 13, 2023 (FoF 3). The lawyers got an extension to March 3 (FoF 4). **March 1, 2023:** Peter LoDuca filed an "Affirmation in Opposition" (ECF 21) citing purported decisions (FoF 5, p. 4). **March 15:** Avianca's reply (ECF 24) said it could not locate most of the cited cases and listed seven by name (FoF 7, p. 5). **April 11 and 12:** the Court ordered LoDuca to file copies of the cited decisions (ECF 25, 27) (FoF 13-14, p. 7). **April 25:** LoDuca filed an affidavit annexing "excerpts" (ECF 29) (FoF 18-19, p. 8). **May 4:** Order to Show Cause (ECF 31). **May 25:** first affidavits admitting ChatGPT (ECF 32, 32-1) (FoF 42-44, p. 18-19). **May 26:** supplemental order (FoF 48). **June 6:** further declarations. **June 8:** sanctions hearing (FoF 50, p. 21). **June 22:** Opinion and Order.
2. **Who did what.** Steven Schwartz, a lawyer at Levidow, Levidow & Oberman who was not admitted in S.D.N.Y., did the research and writing; Peter LoDuca, admitted in the district, signed and filed. LoDuca "reviewed the affirmation for style" and did not read any cited case (FoF 2, 6, pp. 3-4).
3. **What was submitted.** The March 1 filing cited non-existent decisions "said to be published in the Federal Reporter, the Federal Supplement and Westlaw" (FoF 5). The Court identifies six decisions it found to be fake: "Varghese", "Miller", "Petersen", "Shaboon", "Martinez", "Durden" (FoF 36, p. 15; Order to Show Cause p. 2-3). "Varghese" was attributed to an 11th Circuit panel; the Clerk confirmed no such case, and the docket number and reporter cite belonged to other cases (FoF 25-28, pp. 10-11). Several citations within "Varghese" were also fake (FoF 29, pp. 11-13).
4. **When they were told the cases did not exist.** Avianca's March 15 reply said so (FoF 7). The Court says no respondent then sought to withdraw the filing or explain (FoF 8, p. 5). LoDuca testified he did not read the reply and forwarded it to Schwartz (FoF 10, p. 6). Schwartz read it but testified he thought "ChatGPT is finding that case somewhere" (FoF 11, p. 6). He also testified that when he searched for "Varghese" he "couldn't find it", yet it remained cited (FoF 12, p. 6). The court notes this suggests he knew before the March 1 filing, or at the latest before April 25 (fn. 3, p. 7).
5. **What the declarations said, as the Court describes them.** LoDuca's April 25 affidavit said the attached material "may not be inclusive of the entire opinions but only what is made available by online database" without naming a database (FoF 19, p. 8); the Court found LoDuca did not author it and knew nothing of its truth (FoF 20, p. 9). Schwartz's May 25 affidavit said ChatGPT was used "to supplement" research; at the hearing he admitted it was his only substantive source after Fastcase failed (FoF 41, pp. 17-18). The May 25 affidavit also presented ChatGPT screenshots as assurance of reliability; his June 6 declaration offered a different explanation, and the Court found the shifting accounts undermined his credibility (FoF 46-47, pp. 19-20). Schwartz also testified he thought "F.3d" meant "federal district, third department", which the Court found "not credible" (FoF 21 and fn. 6, p. 9).
6. **A false statement to the Court.** LoDuca asked for an extension saying he was "out of the office on vacation"; the Court found this knowingly false (it was Schwartz who was away) and used it as evidence of bad faith (FoF 16-17, pp. 7-8).
7. **How ChatGPT was used.** Schwartz had not used it before, assumed it was "a super search engine", and began by asking for argument that bankruptcy tolls the limitations period, then prompts such as "show me more cases"; ChatGPT "complied by making them up". The firm used Fastcase, which had limited federal coverage (FoF 37-39, pp. 15-17). Schwartz's prompt history is ECF 46-1.
8. **The Court's finding on bad faith.** Findings of subjective bad faith against both individuals, based on "acts of conscious avoidance and false and misleading statements to the Court" (Opinion intro, p. 2; CoL 21-24, pp. 29-31). The Court rests this on the facts after the March 15 reply, not on use of an AI tool.
9. **Sanctions.** Under Rule 11 and, alternatively, the court's inherent power (CoL 27, p. 31): a **$5,000** penalty paid into the Court registry, imposed jointly and severally on LoDuca, Schwartz and the firm; letters to Mata and to each judge named as author of a fake opinion, attaching the Opinion, hearing transcript and April 25 affirmation, within 14 days; copies filed with the Court. No section 1927 sanction (CoL 26); no required apology, "a compelled apology is not a sincere apology" (CoL 33); no fee award because Avianca did not ask (CoL 31). (Conclusion, p. 34; CoL 26-34, pp. 31-33.)
10. **The Court's reasoning on attorneys' role.** Opening paragraph: there is "nothing inherently improper about using a reliable artificial intelligence tool for assistance", but "existing rules impose a gatekeeping role on attorneys to ensure the accuracy of their filings" (Opinion, p. 1). Rule 11 imposes a continuing duty, including when "later advocating" a position after learning it lacks merit (CoL 10, pp. 24-25). A fake opinion "is not 'existing law'" (CoL 9, p. 24).
11. **Mitigating facts the Court recorded.** The firm arranged CLE on AI and notarization practices; the Court found low likelihood of repetition and that the fakes were not submitted for gain (CoL 30-32, pp. 32-33).
12. **Harms listed.** Opposing party's time and money, court time, possible deprivation of the client's real arguments, harm to named judges' reputations, and cynicism about the legal profession (Opinion, pp. 1-2).

### 3. Does the source support the slate's description and angle?

- **Slate description** (Judge Castel's June 22, 2023 order sanctioned the lawyers and firm $5,000, required notices to the client and falsely named judges; nothing inherently improper in AI use, but attorneys have a gatekeeping role): **supported** (receipts 9, 10). The reporter citation 678 F. Supp. 3d 443 was not verified from this copy.
- **Angle** (Haraway's model that "presents every answer with the same confident tone"): **supported as a link, but the opinion's emphasis is different.** The opinion records ChatGPT affirming the cases were real (FoF 45, p. 19; Schwartz's account FoF 47, p. 20), which fits the confident-tone point. But its holding rests on lawyers' conscious avoidance and false statements, not on the model's tone (receipt 8).
- **Fit to the channel premise** (where the knowing sat): **partly.** The knowing that the cases did not exist was available to Avianca's counsel (March 15), the Court, and the lawyers' own search (Schwartz "couldn't find" Varghese); the filing chain (author not signer; signer not reading) put it in front of nobody who would act on it. This is a verification-chain failure, which suits the premise, but the Court frames it as personal bad faith after warnings, not as a systemic gap. An episode should not call it simply "AI hallucination".

### 4. Differences from the book's telling

The book's chapter 5 does **not** discuss Mata v. Avianca; the closest text is in the Haraway section: "An AI that says 'I don't know this domain' is more trustworthy than one that presents every answer with the same confident tone regardless of whether it is summarizing verified research or fabricating a citation." The slate pairs the case with this passage. Nothing in the opinion contradicts it, and nothing in the opinion mentions Haraway or "situated knowledge" (obviously). The opinion does not say the model "presents every answer with the same confident tone"; that is the book's characterisation and the opinion supplies the example (FoF 45).
