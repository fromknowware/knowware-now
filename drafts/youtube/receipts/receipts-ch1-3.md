### 2. Receipts

1. **N.** The paper's Significance statement and body ¶5 both give N = 689,003 for the experiment. (Significance; body ¶5.)
2. **Window.** "The experiments took place for 1 wk (January 11–18, 2012)." (Body ¶7.)
3. **What was done to users: omission, not addition.** Two parallel experiments, one reducing friends' positive posts in the News Feed and one reducing negative posts; each emotional post of the relevant kind had a 10% to 90% chance (set by User ID) of being left out of a given News Feed load. The paper adds that the content stayed available on friends' timelines and direct messages were unaffected. Each experiment had a control that omitted a similar share of posts at random. (Body ¶5 and ¶6.)
4. **Subject counts.** About 155,000 participants per condition "who posted at least one status update" in the window; over 3 million posts and over 122 million words analysed. (Body ¶7, ¶8.) My arithmetic, not the paper's: four conditions of about 155,000 is about 620,000, and one test statistic in body ¶9 has 620,587 degrees of freedom. The paper does not, in the text I read, explain how 689,003 relates to the roughly 155,000 per condition. Ask the author of the explainer to state N the way the paper does and not to equate it with the number analysed.
5. **How emotion was measured.** A post counted as positive or negative if it contained at least one positive or negative word from the LIWC2007 word list; the outcome was the percentage of a person's words that were positive or negative in their own status updates. The authors call this "our operationalization of emotional contagion". (Body ¶6, ¶8, ¶9.) The paper says LIWC ran inside the filtering system "such that no text was seen by the researchers". (Body ¶6.) No survey or other direct measure of mood is reported in the text I read.
6. **Consent, as the paper puts it.** The only consent sentence in the paper: the work "was consistent with Facebook's Data Use Policy", which all users agree to before creating an account, "constituting informed consent for this research". (Body ¶6.) The article text I read contains no sentence about an ethics board; the board statement appears only in the Expression of Concern, where it is quoted from what the authors told PNAS at submission (receipt 9).
7. **Effect sizes reported.** Positive posts reduced: the paper writes B = −0.1% for the share of positive words in people's updates vs control (Cohen's d = 0.02) and negative words rose by 0.04 (d = 0.001). Negative posts reduced: negative words fell by 0.07 (d = 0.02) and positive words rose by 0.06 (d = 0.008). Omitting emotional posts also lowered overall word output (a "withdrawal effect"). (Body ¶10 and ¶11 for the posting-rate and regression results; body ¶14 for withdrawal.) Mean levels for scale: 3.6% of all words were positive and 1.6% negative. (Body ¶8.)
8. **What the authors claim.** They write that the results "show emotional contagion" and describe them as, to their knowledge, the first experimental evidence for massive-scale contagion via social networks (body ¶12). Later they call the results "some of the first" experimental evidence for a "controversial" claim and say the effect sizes are small, "as small as d = 0.001", while arguing that small effects matter at Facebook's scale (body ¶15). The word "unequivocally" does not appear in the paper text I read.
9. **Expression of Concern (July 3, 2014).** PNAS says questions were raised about informed consent and opt-out. It quotes the authors' Data Use Policy sentence, and quotes the authors' statement at submission that, because the experiment was run by Facebook for internal purposes, the Cornell IRB found it fell outside Cornell's Human Research Protection Program. It says Cornell has since confirmed this. It then says (paraphrase) that the Common Rule is PNAS policy and informed consent and opt-out are best practice, that Facebook as a private company was not obliged to follow the Common Rule when collecting the data, that the editors on the authors' information thought publication appropriate, and that it is "a matter of concern" that Facebook's data collection may not have been fully consistent with informed consent and opt-out. (EoC, second paragraph.)
10. **Cornell's own statement (June 30, 2014).** Hancock and Guillory (then a Cornell doctoral student, since at UCSF) "analyzed results from previously conducted research by Facebook", did not take part in data collection and had no access to user data; their work was initial discussions, analysis of results and preparing the paper with Facebook colleagues. Because Facebook ran the research and Hancock saw only results, Cornell's IRB concluded he was "not directly engaged in human research" and no Cornell review was required. (Cornell statement ¶1–2.)
11. **Who did what.** Author contributions line: all three designed the research and wrote the paper; Kramer performed the research and analysed the data. Kramer's affiliation is the Core Data Science Team, Facebook, Inc.; Guillory and Hancock are in Communication and Information Science at Cornell. The paper was edited by Susan T. Fiske, received for review Oct 23, 2013, approved Mar 25, 2014, and declares no conflict of interest. UCSF appears only as Guillory's present address in a footnote. (Author block and notes; Acknowledgments thank the Facebook News Feed team.)
12. **Facebook's framing in the paper.** The paper says News Feed filtering uses a ranking algorithm Facebook "continually develops and tests" and describes this study as one such test, of whether posts with emotional content are more engaging. (Body ¶4.)

### 3. Does the source support the slate?

- **Slate's factual description: supported.** N = 689,003 (receipt 1); one week in January 2012 (2); Data Use Policy as consent (6); Cornell IRB outside its program because Facebook ran it (9, 10); editors still found publication appropriate (9).
- **Slate's angle ("three institutions each held a piece, and none was placed to ask whether the users knew"): partly supported.** The documents show each body stating why the consent question was not its own: Facebook ran the study for internal purposes, Cornell's IRB ruled it outside its program and says its authors saw only results, and PNAS says it publishes under the Common Rule as policy but Facebook was not bound by it (9, 10). But "none was placed to ask" is too strong: the authors addressed consent in the paper by asserting the Data Use Policy (6), and the PNAS editors considered the question and published anyway (9). A defensible line is "each institution had a stated reason the question was someone else's".
- **Fit with the channel premise.** It fits the accountability strand better than a "lost knowing" story: the knowing here is a consent question that three institutions each placed elsewhere. It is not a failure in the sense of a system breaking.

## 2B. The May 6, 2010 Flash Crash (Chapter 2, slot B)

### 1. Document and how it was read

*Findings Regarding the Market Events of May 6, 2010: Report of the Staffs of the CFTC and SEC to the Joint Advisory Committee on Emerging Regulatory Issues*, U.S. CFTC and U.S. SEC staffs, September 30, 2010. https://www.sec.gov/files/marketevents-report.pdf . 104 pages (pdfinfo). The cover states the Commissions "have expressed no view" on the analysis, findings or conclusions, which are staff's.

Downloaded the PDF, extracted text with pdftotext, and read: the Executive Summary (pp. 1–8) in full; Section I.4 (pp. 13–16); Section II.2.a and II.2.b (pp. 35–39); Section III.3 (pp. 76–79). **Not read:** Sections I.1–I.3, I.5–I.6, II.2.c–f (ETFs, HFT equity firms, internalizers, options), II.3, III.1–III.2, all of Section IV (order books), and the footnotes outside those pages. Page numbers below are the report's printed page numbers.

### 2. Receipts

1. **Backdrop.** Major indices were already down over 4% from the prior day's close before the further 5–6% plunge. By 2:30 p.m. the DJIA was down about 2.5% and the VIX up 22.5%. Buy-side depth in the E-Mini had fallen from about $6 billion to $2.65 billion (55%) and in SPY from about $275 million to $220 million (20%). (Exec. Summary, pp. 1–2.)
2. **The sale.** At 2:32 p.m. "a large fundamental trader (a mutual fund complex)" began a program to sell 75,000 June E-Mini contracts, about $4.1 billion, as a hedge to an existing equity position. The report does not name the trader. (Exec. Summary, p. 2; §I.4, p. 14.)
3. **The execution algorithm.** The trader chose an automated "Sell Algorithm" set to feed orders at 9% of the previous minute's trading volume, "without regard to price or time". The report says that on the prior comparable sale the same trader used a mix of manual trading and algorithms that took price, time and volume into account and took more than five hours for the first 75,000 contracts; on May 6 the sale took about 20 minutes. Footnote 24 notes that other algorithm types (e.g. VWAP) have a built-in time throttle or limit price. (Exec. Summary, p. 2; §I.4, p. 14 and fn. 24.)
4. **Size relative to the market.** The program was the largest net change in daily position of any E-Mini trader from Jan 1 to May 6, 2010. (Exec. Summary, p. 2.)
5. **Feedback between algorithm and HFTs.** HFTs and intermediaries were the likely buyers of the first orders and built a net long of about 3,300 contracts, then between 2:41 and 2:44 p.m. sold about 2,000 to cut it, while trading nearly 140,000 contracts (over 33% of volume). The Sell Algorithm "responded to the increased volume by increasing the rate" of its orders although earlier orders were arguably not yet absorbed. The report's lesson: "high trading volume is not necessarily a reliable indicator of market liquidity". (Exec. Summary, pp. 3, 6; §I.4, p. 15.)
6. **Price and depth collapse in the E-Mini.** The E-Mini fell about 3% in four minutes (2:41–2:44 p.m.). Between 2:45:13 and 2:45:27 HFTs traded over 27,000 contracts (about 49% of volume) while buying only about 200 net, the report's "hot-potato" effect. Buy-side depth dropped to about $58 million (under 1% of the morning level); price fell a further 1.7% in those 15 seconds to an intraday low of 1056; over 4.5 minutes the E-Mini fell more than 5% and SPY over 6%. (Exec. Summary, pp. 3–4; §I.4, p. 15.)
7. **The pause that stopped it.** At 2:45:28 p.m. the CME Stop Logic Functionality paused E-Mini trading for five seconds; trading resumed at 2:45:33 and prices stabilised. By 2:45 the algorithm had sold about 35,000 of the 75,000 contracts and it finished the rest by about 2:51 p.m. while prices rose. The report says none of the market-wide circuit breakers triggered. (Exec. Summary, pp. 4, 6; §I.4, p. 16.)
8. **Second crisis, in individual stocks.** Interviews with market participants indicate that many liquidity providers' automated systems paused after the sudden declines; each firm then had to judge whether the prices could be erroneous data, what the moves did to risk limits and P&L, whether trades might be broken, and whether its systems could cope. Some reported fearing a "cataclysmic event of which they were not yet aware". Market makers widened spreads, cut size or withdrew; some went manual but could cover only a subset of securities because volume rose nearly ten-fold. (Exec. Summary, pp. 4–5.)
9. **Pauses feeding pauses.** Firms told staff data integrity was their "number one concern" and many systems stop automatically on large, rapid price moves ("price-driven integrity pauses"); others paused on conflicting feeds ("feed-driven"). The report says the declines were real, not data errors, and describes a loop: when one firm pauses, others absorb the flow, which can cause more rapid moves and more pauses. (§II.2.a, pp. 35–36.)
10. **Market makers.** Market makers reported internal risk limits breached around 2:45 p.m., widening quotes or pausing; their monitoring "continuously triggered visual and audio alarms"; most needed human intervention to re-enter and re-entry took from seconds to hours. Where they stopped quoting, stub quotes (far-from-market quotes that satisfy two-sided quoting obligations) remained. (§II.2.b, pp. 37–38.)
11. **Outcome.** Between 2:40 and 3:00 p.m. about 2 billion shares traded (over $56 billion) with over 98% of shares executed within 10% of their 2:40 price, but over 20,000 trades in more than 300 securities executed at prices 60% or more from 2:40 prices, as low as a penny and as high as $100,000, via stub quotes; all were cancelled after the close under the "clearly erroneous" rules. (Exec. Summary, pp. 5–6.)
12. **Data, and what staff say about seeing the whole market.** NYSE delays: for 1,665 symbols the consolidated quote feed lagged by an average of over 10 seconds between 2:44:45 and 2:46:29, while NYSE's proprietary feed lagged about 8 milliseconds; many large firms use proprietary feeds. Staff conclude the evidence "does not support" the hypothesis that the consolidated-feed delays caused the extreme volatility (§III.3, pp. 77–79). In the Executive Summary staff note that many data sources "must be aggregated" to give a complete picture of the markets, and that varied conventions, volume and physical time lags add complexity (p. 8). **The report does not say that nobody could see the whole market in real time.** What it documents is that individual firms acted on their own checks and data (receipts 8–10) and that the staff's picture is a reconstruction from order-level data plus interviews with more than three dozen firms (§II.2.a, fn. 30, p. 35).

### 3. Does the source support the slate?

- **Description ("many automated participants each acting sensibly while nobody could see the whole market"): partly supported.** The first half is documented: automated pauses and risk limits that each made sense for the firm, and a collective liquidity crisis when many withdrew at once (receipts 8–10; report p. 6 lesson that single withdrawals matter little but simultaneous ones can). The second half ("nobody could see the whole market") is the slate's gloss; the report supports only the narrower points in receipt 12 (data aggregation burden; some participants unsure of data; staff reconstructed the picture afterwards).
- **Where the knowing sat.** The report puts the decisive choice with a human decision: the trader's choice of a volume-only algorithm without price or time limits (receipt 3), with no statement that anyone at the seller was watching for liquidity. It does not say what the seller knew or monitored during the 20 minutes; **unknown**.
- **Fit with the channel premise: partial.** This is more a market-structure and interaction failure than a case where the knowing was held by someone and not passed on. If the episode is kept, the better-supported angle is the algorithm's missing price and time throttle, plus every participant's own pause rule. Another case in the slate's own alternates list (LTCM, Columbia) was not researched by me, so I cannot say it is better.
- I read nothing later than the report, including any later legal action about May 6; the report says nothing about it.

### 4. Differences from the book

The book (Chapter 2, read from chapter-02.md) does not discuss the Flash Crash. Its high-frequency trading passage is an anonymous "savant" describing markets as coordination systems among price discovery, liquidity and information; it makes no claim that can be checked against this report. No difference to record.

## 2C. Google Flu Trends (Chapter 2, slot C)

### 1. Document and how it was read

David Lazer, Ryan Kennedy, Gary King, Alessandro Vespignani, "The Parable of Google Flu: Traps in Big Data Analysis", *Science* 343(6176):1203–1205, March 14, 2014. doi 10.1126/science.1248506. Open copy from Harvard DASH, downloaded 2026-10-17 from https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-0e0d-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content (handle http://nrs.harvard.edu/urn-3:HUL.InstRepos:12016836). I extracted its text and read all of it (nine PDF pages including a repository cover sheet): body, references, acknowledgments and the Fig. 1 caption. **Not read:** the Supplementary Materials (the paper cites an "SM"), and the figure itself. The copy has no Science page numbers, so locators are the paper's section headings and paragraph counts.

### 2. Receipts

1. **The trigger.** In February 2013, *Nature* reported that GFT predicted more than double the proportion of doctor visits for influenza-like illness (ILI) that the CDC's surveillance reports showed, though GFT "was built to predict CDC reports". (Opening paragraph.)
2. **Weeks of error.** GFT "missed high for 100 out of 108 weeks starting with August 2011". The Fig. 1 caption says GFT overestimated 2012–13 and overshot 2011–12 by more than 50%, and gives out-of-sample mean absolute error of 0.486 for GFT, 0.311 for a lagged-CDC model and 0.232 for GFT combined with lagged CDC data. (Section "Big Data Hubris", ¶4; Fig. 1 caption.)
3. **The overfitting argument.** The paper describes the original method as finding best matches "among 50 million search terms to fit 1152 data points", so the odds were "quite high" of finding terms that match flu in the data but are structurally unrelated. It points to the developers weeding out seasonal terms such as high school basketball and says this "should have been a warning" of overfitting. (Section "Big Data Hubris", ¶2.)
4. **"Part flu detector, part winter detector."** The paper says the ad hoc term-removal failed when GFT missed the non-seasonal 2009 H1N1 pandemic; GFT engineers updated the algorithm in 2009, and the model has run since with a few changes announced October 2013. (Section "Big Data Hubris", ¶3.)
5. **Errors have structure.** Last week's errors predict this week's (autocorrelation) and error varies with time of year, so GFT "overlooks considerable information" that standard methods could use. (Section "Big Data Hubris", ¶4.)
6. **Simple benchmark.** A 2010 study found GFT no more accurate than a simple projection from CDC data on a two-week lag; the authors say lagged models now "significantly" outperform GFT, and that even 3-week-old CDC data do better. (Section "Big Data Hubris", ¶5.) Combining GFT with lagged CDC data and recalibration improves on either alone, and "GFT could have largely healed itself". (Same section, ¶6.)
7. **Algorithm dynamics and "blue team" dynamics.** The authors say it is "quite likely" GFT was unstable because of changes by Google's engineers and by users: they cite 86 changes to Google search reported for June–July 2012 alone, and Google announcements in June 2011 (suggested related searches) and February 2012 (potential diagnoses shown for symptom searches). They state that searches for flu treatments and for telling cold from flu "track closely" with GFT's errors, which "points to the possibility" of blue-team dynamics, meaning the service provider changing the data-generating process for its business model. The language is hedged throughout ("likely", "possibility"); it is not a proven cause. (Section "Algorithm Dynamics", ¶1–5; evidence from Google Correlate is in the unread SM.)
8. **Media panic is not enough.** The commonly cited explanation, media-stoked panic in the last flu season, "cannot explain" why GFT missed high for over two years, and the 2009 version weathered the 2005–06 bird-flu and 2009 swine-flu media periods. (Section "Algorithm Dynamics", ¶1.)
9. **Replication.** GFT "has never documented the 45 search terms used", the few released terms look misleading, and the supporting materials "did not meet emerging community standards". Lazer et al. say they "surmise" that the GFT authors "felt an unarticulated need to cloak the actual search terms" (their inference, not a documented fact). (Section "Algorithm Dynamics", ¶2; "Transparency and Replicability".)
10. **Recommendations.** (a) Transparency and replicability: release derivative, aggregated data. (b) Use big data to understand the unknown: since a lagged model is already good, the value is finer geography than the CDC can publish. (c) Study the algorithm: replicate across time and sources, and run controlled experiments with Google. (d) Not just size: "all data", combining new and traditional sources. They also call on universities to build models for collaboration with big-data firms. (Section "Transparency, Granularity, and All-Data".)
11. **Red team.** The authors say red-team manipulation (users gaming the signal) "does not appear to be an issue in GFT". (Section "Algorithm Dynamics", ¶7.)
12. **Google's response: not reported in this paper.** The paper cites Google's own papers and blog posts (refs 11, 13–15, 21, 22) for what Google published and changed, but it contains no statement from Google responding to the paper's critique. Whether Google replied elsewhere is **unknown** to me; I did not search for it.

### 3. Does the source support the slate?

- **Slate's description (50 million terms to 1,152 data points; large errors; "winter detector"): supported** (receipts 3, 4). The slate's second claim that "large errors were largely avoidable" came from search summaries; the paper's actual claim is narrower: GFT "could have largely healed itself" with lagged CDC data and recalibration (receipt 6).
- **Slate's angle ("association (Pearl's rung one) mistaken for cause"): partly supported, with a different mechanism.** The paper's two arguments are overfitting of a very large search-term space to a small number of data points (receipt 3) and drift in the measuring instrument as Google changed its search service (receipt 7). It never uses causal-inference language or Pearl's ladder. Calling the spurious term matches "association mistaken for cause" is the slate's interpretation of receipt 3, not a statement of the paper.
- **Fit with the channel premise: weak to partial.** Nobody "held the knowing" and failed to pass it on in a documented way. The paper's nearest points are (i) the people who built GFT did not document the terms, so outsiders could not check it (receipt 9) and (ii) the search engineers' changes plausibly altered the signal without the flu team necessarily knowing (receipt 7, hedged; the paper does not say who knew what inside Google). The authors are critics; their account is not balanced by a Google reply (receipt 12). If the episode is kept, the safest framing is "an instrument whose maker kept changing it and never published how it worked". If a stronger fit is wanted, Challenger and Therac already cover Chapter 3; for Chapter 2 the slate's unread alternates (LTCM, Columbia) are options but I have no source for them.

### 4. Differences from the book

The book's Chapter 2 does not mention Google Flu Trends. Pearl's ladder is explained at the general level (association, intervention, counterfactual) with unrelated examples (diapers and beer, smokers and lung cancer). Nothing to compare.

## 3A. Challenger (Chapter 3, slot A)

### 1. Documents and how they were read

*Report of the Presidential Commission on the Space Shuttle Challenger Accident* (Rogers Commission), June 1986, NASA History Division HTML edition, https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/ . Downloaded and extracted as text on 2026-10-17:

- Vol. I Chapter I through IX (v1ch1 to v1ch9.htm). **Read in detail:** Ch. III opening paragraph; Ch. IV Findings (items 6 and 12 only); Ch. V (opening finding, "Flaws in the Decision Making Process", the waiver passage, the 26–27 January events, the teleconference testimony of Boisjoly, McDonald, Mulloy, Lund and Mason, Findings 1–4, and the Chronology of Events through the fax). **Searched only, not read in full:** Ch. I, II, VI to IX, and the rest of Ch. IV.
- Vol. II, Appendix F, R. P. Feynman, "Personal Observations on Reliability of Shuttle" (v2appf.htm). Read the introduction, the O-ring "safety factor" section, and the concluding paragraphs; searched the rest.
- Vol. IV hearing transcripts (v4part4.htm, Feb 11, 1986; v4part5a.htm, Feb 13, 1986), searched for "ice water" and read around each hit.

The NASA HTML has only sparse page markers (e.g. "[82]"); where a locator says "p." it is taken from those markers, otherwise from section names. Also noted: the DTIC copy (ADA171404) was not downloaded; the report's volume numbering follows the NASA site.

**Correction to the brief.** The slate and brief point to Appendix F for the ice-water demonstration. Appendix F, as I read it, does not contain it (no occurrence of "ice" or "clamp" in its text). The demonstration is in the Vol. IV hearing transcript (receipt 11).

### 2. Receipts

1. **Loss and crew.** Mission 51-L began at 11:38 a.m. EST on January 28, 1986 and "ended 73 seconds later"; "All seven crew members perished." (Vol. I Ch. III, opening paragraph.)
2. **Launch conditions.** The ambient temperature at launch was 36°F, 15°F lower than the next coldest previous launch; the right aft field joint at the coldest point was estimated at 28°F ±5°F, while the sun-facing side of the right booster was estimated at about 50°F. Of 21 launches at 61°F or above, only four showed O-ring thermal distress; every launch below 61°F showed distress in one or more O-rings. (Ch. IV Findings 6 and 12, pp. 70–71 by the markers.)
3. **The Commission's headline finding.** "The decision to launch the Challenger was flawed." Those who made it were unaware of the recent O-ring history, of Thiokol's initial written recommendation against launching below 53°F, and of the continuing opposition of Thiokol engineers after management reversed its position. The Commission says that had the decision-makers known all the facts it is "highly unlikely" they would have launched on January 28. (Ch. V, opening paragraph, p. 82.)
4. **Prior waivers.** After erosion of a secondary O-ring on flight 51-B in July 1985, a launch constraint was placed on 51-F and later flights, and it was regularly waived by the SRB Project Manager at Marshall, Lawrence Mulloy. Neither the constraint, its reason, nor the six consecutive waivers were known to Moore (Level I) or Aldrich (Level II) when the Flight Readiness Review for 51-L was held. (Ch. V, "Flaws in the Decision Making Process", about p. 84.) The O-ring problem is also reported as absent from the Certification of Flight Readiness signed January 9 and the endorsement signed January 15, 1986 by Kilminster and by Mulloy. (Ch. V, same section.)
5. **Cold-weather lead-up.** At a Mission Management Team meeting at 2:00 p.m. on January 27 the cold was discussed for the launch facility, "no concerns were expressed about the O-rings". At about 2:30 p.m. at Thiokol's Wasatch plant, Robert Ebeling called a meeting of engineers including Roger Boisjoly on learning of the forecast; Marshall's Cecil Houston then asked Lovingood to set up a teleconference. (Ch. V, pp. 85–86 area.)
6. **First teleconference phase, 5:45 p.m. EST.** Participants included Reinartz, Lovingood and Hardy plus people at Kennedy, Marshall and Thiokol; Thiokol presented concerns about low temperature and the O-rings "along with an opinion that launch should be delayed", and a recommendation was made that Aldrich, the Level II program manager, be informed. (Ch. V, about p. 88.)
7. **The engineers' recommendation (second teleconference, evening).** Thiokol's Robert Lund presented charts concluding that the engineers would not recommend launching below 53°F, the lowest previous O-ring temperature on the Thiokol calculation. Mulloy, McDonald and Boisjoly agree on this in testimony. Boisjoly said he was asked to quantify his concern and "couldn't", and that others cited soot blow-by on flight 61-A at 75°F to argue temperature was not the discriminator. (Ch. V, testimony pp. 89–91; Mulloy's testimony at p. 91.)
8. **Pushback from NASA.** Boisjoly and Mason testified that Hardy said he was "appalled" by the recommendation. Mulloy, asked about reports he had said "do you expect us to wait till April", testified that the words he read in the record were "My God, Thiokol, when do you want me to launch, next April?" and gave the context as challenging a new launch criterion proposed on the eve of launch. (Ch. V, pp. 90–95; Mulloy's testimony in the section on the quotes, about p. 96.)
9. **The caucus.** Thiokol went offline for about 30 minutes from about 10:30 p.m. Thompson and Boisjoly objected to launch and Lund was reluctant; a final review was held with only Mason, Lund, Kilminster and Wiggins; Mason asked Lund "to take off his engineering hat and put on his management hat" (Boisjoly's testimony, p. 93; Chronology p. 108). The final rationale recorded: a margin of three times the previous worst erosion, and the secondary O-ring would seal. Lund testified the roles had "switched": instead of Thiokol having to show the motor was ready to fly, they were trying to prove it would not work and could not (Ch. V, about pp. 94–95).
10. **The reversal and who was told.** Thiokol then recommended launch, Kilminster read the rationale, and Hardy asked that it be put in writing and faxed to Kennedy and Marshall (Chronology, pp. 108–109). The Chronology says Aldrich "is apparently not informed" of the O-ring concerns (p. 109). Findings 3 and 4: the Commission is "troubled" by a propensity at Marshall to contain serious problems rather than communicate them forward, and concluded Thiokol management reversed its position "at the urging of Marshall and contrary to the views of its engineers" to accommodate a major customer. (Ch. V Findings, p. 104.)
11. **Feynman's ice water: where it is.** In the Commission's public hearing of **February 11, 1986** (transcript Vol. IV, pp. 679–680, in v4part4.htm, after Mulloy's testimony and before Richard Cook's), Feynman says he took material from the seal and put it in ice water and found that after pressure was applied and released, it did not stretch back, and that for seconds there was "no resilience in this particular material" at 32 degrees. The transcript has no stage directions: it does not mention a clamp, a glass, a pair of pliers or any action beyond his spoken statement. A different remark, on **February 13, 1986** (Vol. IV p. 952, v4part5a.htm), is Feynman asking Hardy about a test where you "take a clamp and you tighten it up in a glass of ice water", met with laughter. I found no description of the demonstration in Volume I (Chapters I–IX) or Appendix F.
12. **What Feynman's Appendix F does say.** Estimates of the probability of loss of vehicle and life range "from roughly 1 in 100 to 1 in 100,000", with the higher figures from working engineers and the very low ones from management; he asks the cause of management's "fantastic faith in the machinery" (App. F, Introduction, [F1]). He criticises the phrase "safety factor of three" as applied to erosion depth ([F2]). He closes that reality must take precedence over public relations ([F5]). Ch. VI, in listing findings, quotes Feynman calling the decision making "a kind of Russian roulette".

### 3. Does the source support the slate?

- **Slate: "the temperature data and the O-ring data were both held; they were not put in front of the same decision": partly supported, and the framing needs changing.** The temperature and O-ring link was in front of a decision on the night of January 27: Thiokol engineers made it, in a teleconference with Marshall and Kennedy participants, and recommended no launch below 53°F (receipts 6–7). What the Commission documents is that it was reversed in a management caucus under pressure (9), that the reversal and the engineers' continuing dissent did not travel up to Level I and II managers (3, 4, 10), and that the data linking temperature to the O-rings were incomplete and contested (7). The best-supported framing: the knowing sat with working engineers, was overruled, and did not reach those who made the final decision.
- **Fit with the channel premise: strong.**
- The slate's remark that Appendix F "is on the NASA history site" is correct, but the demonstration is not in it (see §1 correction).

## 3B. Therac-25 (Chapter 3, slot B)

### 1. Document and how it was read

Nancy Leveson and Clark S. Turner, "An Investigation of the Therac-25 Accidents", *IEEE Computer* 26(7), July 1993, pp. 18–41. Read from the MIT 6.033 reprint "with permission": https://web.mit.edu/6.033/2004/wwwdocs/papers/Therac_1.html through Therac_5.html (Parts I–V), plus the four linked sidebars (Side_bar_1: software development and design; Side_bar_2: major event time line; Side_bar_X: turntable positioning; Side_bar_Y: the operator interface). I read all five parts and all four sidebars in full. **Not read:** the figures (GIFs) and the IEEE print edition. The reprint has no IEEE page numbers, so locators are part (I–V) and heading.

The authors say they used public documents, lawsuits, and US and Canadian regulator files, and that they cannot guarantee the documents are correct (Part I, intro).

### 2. Receipts

1. **Headline figures.** The paper's first paragraph says six known accidents between June 1985 and January 1987 involved massive overdoses by the Therac-25, "with resultant deaths and serious injuries". Eleven machines were installed (five US, six Canada); it was recalled in 1987 for design changes including hardware safeguards against software errors. (Part I, opening paragraph and "Accident history".) **The paper does not give a total death count in any sentence I read.**
2. **Accident-by-accident outcomes as the paper states them.** Kennestone, Marietta, Georgia, June 3, 1985: a 61-year-old woman, breast later removed, lost use of her shoulder and arm, in constant pain; no death stated. Hamilton, Ontario, July 26, 1985: patient died Nov 3, 1985; the autopsy gave "an extremely virulent cancer" as the cause of death, and the paper says a hip replacement would have been needed otherwise. Yakima, Washington, December 1985: patient survived with scarring and minor disability. Tyler (East Texas Cancer Center), March 21, 1986: patient died "from complications of the overdose five months after". Tyler, April 11, 1986: patient died May 1, 1986. Yakima, January 17, 1987: patient died in April "from complications related to the overdose" but had a terminal cancer, and the family's suit alleged he died sooner than he would have. (Part I accident sections; Part II Tyler sections; Part III second Yakima section.) My tally from these passages: the paper attributes deaths to the overdose in three accidents (two Tyler, one Yakima 1987, the last with the cancer caveat) and not in the Hamilton case. The paper itself does not total them.
3. **Software fault, Tyler (March and April 1986).** When an operator edited the prescription's mode or energy quickly (within about 8 seconds, the time the bending magnets take to set), the edit appeared on screen but the machine parameters were not updated, so the beam was set up for the wrong mode; the paper calls the data-entry completion flag a "potential race condition" and says it records only that the cursor had been on the command line, not that it was still there. AECL also found a second problem in the data-entry logic. Dose at Tyler could be in the 16,500 to 25,000 rad range per after-the-fact simulation (March) and over 4,000 rads measured by the hospital physicist, 25,000 by AECL (April); the paper says exact doses cannot be determined. (Part II, "The software problem"; Part III, first paragraphs.)
4. **Software fault, Yakima (January 1987).** A different fault: a one-byte counter, Class3, was incremented each pass through the Set-Up Test routine and wrapped to zero every 256th pass, and on that pass the upper-collimator check was skipped. The overdose occurred when the operator pressed "set" at that moment with the turntable in the field-light position. The paper calls this "a type of race condition". AECL's estimated dose: 4,000 to 5,000 rads per exposure, possibly 8,000 to 10,000 total vs 86 rads prescribed. (Part III, "The Yakima software problem".)
5. **Removed or reduced hardware interlocks.** AECL "decided not to duplicate all the existing hardware safety mechanisms and interlocks" of earlier machines. The Therac-20 had independent protective circuits and mechanical interlocks; the Therac-25 relied more on software. The Therac-20 had the same software error but its hardware interlocks meant nobody was hurt. The wording in the slate ("removed") is looser than the paper's: the Therac-25 was designed from the start around computer control, not built on a stand-alone machine. (Part I, "Genesis of the Therac-25"; Side_bar_X; Part V.)
6. **Safety analysis and development.** AECL's March 1983 fault-tree analysis "apparently excluded the software" and assumed software errors had been reduced by testing; it gave probabilities for "computer selects wrong energy" with no justification. The software was written by one person in PDP-11 assembly, with little documentation; AECL's quality assurance manager described 2,700 "hours of use" as testing; no one could tell the plaintiffs' lawyers the programmer's background. (Part I, "Genesis"; Side_bar_1.)
7. **How the manufacturer learned, and told others.** Marietta: it is "unclear exactly when AECL learned"; a lawsuit was filed Nov 13, 1985 and AECL later said it first received it in March 1986. The accident was not reported to the FDA until after the Tyler accidents; at the time the reporting rules covered manufacturers and importers, not hospitals. Other users did not know. Hamilton: AECL, the FDA and Canada's CRPB were informed of a problem, but users say they were not told a patient had been injured. Yakima 1985: AECL wrote on Feb 24, 1986 that the damage could not have been caused by the Therac-25 and that there had been no similar damage elsewhere. Tyler: the hospital physicist says AECL told him it knew of no other overexposure accident. (Part I, Kennestone and Hamilton sections; Part II, Yakima and Tyler "User and manufacturer response".)
8. **How the regulators learned.** The first reports of the Tyler accidents reached the FDA from the Texas health department. AECL's accident report followed (April 15, 1986). On May 2, 1986 the FDA declared the machine defective, and told AECL its April 15 letter to users (disable the cursor-up key) did not describe the defect or its hazards. (Part III, "Government and user response".)
9. **What the FDA and users said to each other.** Users formed a group and complained of a "lack of information propagation"; the AECL representative said AECL did not want to spread rumours and had no policy to keep things quiet. FDA internal memos over autumn 1986 asked whether AECL knew earlier of the problems and noted an "apparent lack of documentation on software specifications and a software test plan". On Feb 10, 1987 the FDA issued a Notice of Adverse Findings, and in Feb 1987 the FDA concluded software alone could not be relied on. (Part III; Part IV; the quotes are in those parts.)
10. **The fix sequence.** An initial corrective action plan (June 13, 1986) and five revisions, the last on July 21, 1987; the FDA granted a Class I recall rating (May 26, 1987); the final plan added software and hardware single-pulse shutdown, a turntable position interlock, a potentiometer, restricted edit keys and meaningful error messages. The operator interface let a pause be overridden with "P" up to five times; operators were used to frequent malfunctions and cryptic "Malfunction" numbers that the manuals did not explain. (Part IV; Side_bar_Y; Part II for Tyler.)
11. **The paper's lessons.** "Fixing each individual software flaw as it was found did not solve" the safety problems (Part V, near the end). Safety is a property of the whole system, not of the software, and reused software does not guarantee safety (Part V, "Software engineering"). Manufacturers should not "keep government agencies and users in the dark" (Part V, "User and government oversight"). It was users who found the problems and forced AECL to respond (same). The authors warn that blaming a single software error is misleading; accidents come from organisational, managerial, technical and political factors.
12. **User-to-user flow.** Therac-20 users heard "informally" about the Tyler accidents; a University of Chicago physicist found the same bug in a Therac-20, which had been blowing fuses whenever new students used the machine, and told the FDA, which notified Therac-20 users. (Part II, "Related Therac-20 problems".)

### 3. Does the source support the slate?

- **Slate: "six known accidents between June 1985 and January 1987 involving massive overdoses": supported** (receipt 1).
- **Slate: "a race-condition-style fault in how operator edits and beam setup interacted": supported for Tyler (receipt 3), but there are two distinct faults.** Yakima 1987 was a counter overflow that skipped a check (receipt 4), not the edit-timing bug. For Kennestone, Hamilton and the first Yakima accident the paper says it cannot determine which fault was involved.
- **Slate: "hardware interlocks from the earlier machine were removed": partly supported.** The paper says AECL chose not to duplicate them (receipt 5).
- **Slate's angle ("two correct programs...incorrect results", the book's concurrency passage): partly supported at most.** The paper does describe concurrent tasks sharing variables and calls the Tyler fault a race condition, but it describes the software as poorly engineered and unsafe in design, not as two correct programs. Using the book's "both threads are individually correct" line for this case would not match the paper.
- **Fit with the channel premise: strong, and the stronger angle is information flow.** The paper documents hospitals, the manufacturer and two regulators each holding part of the picture and not sharing it (receipts 7–9, 12), and says the fixing process was user-driven (11).

### 4. Differences from the book

Chapter 3 (read from chapter-03.md) does not name Therac-25. Its concurrency passage (bank balance example; "Both threads are individually correct") is a general illustration, and the same chapter names Heartbleed, the Morris worm and Code Red in a separate list. Nothing in the book's text about Therac-25 to compare; the slate's link between that passage and this case is the slate's own.
