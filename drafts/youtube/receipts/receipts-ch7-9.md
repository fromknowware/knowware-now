## 7C. The Walkway: Hyatt Regency Kansas City, July 17, 1981

### 1. Primary document and how I read it

- **What the slate cites:** NBS report NBSIR 82-2465A (Feb 1982), plus companion NBS Building Science Series 143.
- **What those two files actually are (checked):**
  - `nbsir82-2465A.pdf` (110 pages) is the **appendix volume** only. Its cover reads "NBSIR 82-2465 (A)", February 1982. After the abstract it holds load-displacement figures and materials tests (sections A6.10, A7.2 to A7.5). It has none of the narrative the episode needs.
  - **NBS Building Science Series 143**, "Investigation of the Kansas City Hyatt Regency Walkways Collapse", R. D. Marshall, E. O. Pfrang, E. V. Leyendecker, K. A. Woodward (Center for Building Technology) with R. P. Reed, M. B. Kasen, T. R. Shives (Center for Materials Science), National Bureau of Standards, **issued May 1982**, is the full report (378 PDF pages; the cover page says 360). Its abstract says supplementary material is "published as NBSIR 82-2465A". This is the document I cite below as "BSS 143".
- **URLs:** https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/BSS/nbsbuildingscience143.pdf and https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nbsir82-2465A.pdf (both downloaded with curl, text extracted with pdftotext).
- **What I read in BSS 143:** abstract (p. iii), executive summary (pp. v to vii), table of contents, chapter 1 (introduction), sections 2.2 to 2.8 (documents reviewed), 3.3, 3.4, 3.6, 4.2 and 4.4, all of chapter 10, all of chapter 11. **Not read:** chapters 5 to 9 (site investigation, structural testing, materials, fractography, structural analysis) and the appendix beyond its first pages. I rely only on the summaries in chapters 10 and 11 for their results.
- **Later primary record:** see receipts 13 to 17 below (Missouri Court of Appeals opinion read in part; the Administrative Hearing Commission decision itself not obtained).

### 2. Receipts

**NBS report (BSS 143)**

1. **Event.** About 7:05 p.m. on July 17, 1981, two suspended walkways (second and fourth floor) in the hotel atrium collapsed; the fourth-floor walkway came to rest on the second-floor one. The hotel had been in service about a year. The third-floor walkway, hung independently, did not fall. (§1.1, p. 1; Executive Summary p. v.)
2. **Casualty figures, as the report states them.** Executive Summary, p. v: collapse left 113 people dead and 186 injured. §1.1, p. 1 and §4.2, p. 34: 111 killed and 188 injured as reported by July 19, "Two of the injured subsequently died." (Mine: 111 + 2 = 113 and 188 − 2 = 186, so the two statements are consistent.) The §4.2 timeline and the 111/188 figure come from the Kansas City Times and Star (§4.2, p. 33), not from NBS's own count.
3. **Original design.** The plans approved by the Kansas City Codes Administration Office called for one set of continuous hanger rods running from the roof framing through the fourth-floor box beams and on through the second-floor box beams, each box beam resting on a washer and nut. (Executive Summary p. vi; §10.5, p. 246.)
4. **As built.** The fabricator's shop drawings instead used two sets of rods: fourth floor to roof, and second floor to fourth floor. The second set was offset 4 in. NBS says so three times: Executive Summary p. vi; §3.3, p. 15; §3.6 item 4, p. 17. The washer-and-nut arrangement of the original connection was retained (§10.5, p. 246).
5. **Who reviewed the shop drawings, as the report documents it.** The shop drawings were prepared by Havens Steel Company, dated between January 7 and February 9, 1979, and "reviewed, as indicated by their stamps" by the contractor, structural engineer and architect (§2.5, p. 7; Executive Summary p. vi). Roles (§2.2, p. 5): Crown Center Redevelopment Corporation, owner; Hyatt Hotels Corporation, operator; PBNDML, architect; Gillum-Colaco Consulting Structural Engineers, structural engineer; Concordia Project Management, construction manager; Eldridge & Son Construction, general contractor; Havens Steel Company, fabricator. **The report does not give the date of any stamp, does not name any individual reviewer, and says nothing about why the change was made or who proposed it.**
6. **No calls are documented.** I searched the full text of BSS 143 for "phone", "telephon", "call" and "communicat": no passage describes calls or meetings between the fabricator and the engineers about the rod change. The slate's wish for "calls between the fabricator and engineers, as the report documents them" cannot be met from NBS; the only account I found is in the court opinion (receipt 14).
7. **Missing records.** NBS says it especially wanted the original structural design calculations and any change orders, but efforts by the Mayor's office to get them "were not successful" (§2.7, p. 10; the quoted phrase is paraphrase-length only). The report also records that the owner's inspection agency's report on bolted-connection inspections of August 17 and 20, 1979 said the hanging walkway connections were not checked with a calibrated torque wrench because they were inaccessible (§2.6, p. 9). The architect's daily logs have "very little specific information" on the walkways (§2.6, p. 8).
8. **The load comparison, as stated.**
   - Continuous-rod arrangement: design load per box beam–hanger rod connection about 20.3 kips (90 kN).
   - Interrupted-rod arrangement as built: 40.7 kips (181 kN) at a fourth-floor connection. The report says the change "essentially doubled" the load (§10.5, p. 246; §11 item 6, p. 255).
   - Expected ultimate capacity of a connection designed under the AISC specification, which underlies the Kansas City Building Code: at least 68 kips (1.67 × 40.7). NBS's test-based estimate of actual mean ultimate capacity of the six fourth-floor connections: 18.2 to 19.3 kips, average 18.6 kips (§10.3, pp. 244 to 245; §11 item 5(c), p. 255).
   - Under the original continuous-rod detail the expected ultimate was at least 33.9 kips against about 20.5 kips tested, "approximately 60 percent" (§10.5, p. 246).
9. **Load at the time of collapse.** Estimated maximum load on the most heavily loaded fourth-floor connection (location 8UE): 21.4 kips (18.9 dead plus 2.5 live). That is "53 percent of what was required for design" (§10.2, pp. 243 to 244; §11 item 1(d), p. 254). The abstract (p. iii) states the same comparison against ultimate capacity as 31 percent (mine: 21.4 ÷ 68 ≈ 31%). So the 53% and 31% figures are two different denominators, not a discrepancy. Credible upper-bound occupancy of the two walkways: 63 people, based on a KMBC TV videotape (§4.4, pp. 36 to 37; §11 item 1(a)). Actual dead load was about 8 percent above nominal (§11 item 1(b)).
10. **Conclusion on cause, in the report's words.** Most probable cause: insufficient load capacity of the box beam–hanger rod connections (abstract, p. iii). Two contributing factors: the original connection design was inadequate (identical on all three walkways), and the rod change doubled the load on the fourth-floor connections. Failure most likely began at the east end of the fourth-floor middle box beam, location 9UE (Executive Summary conclusion 4; §10.4, p. 245).
11. **The original design was not safe either.** Even without the change, the connection "would not have satisfied" the Building Code (§11 item 7, p. 255), and the third-floor walkway, which was never changed, would have been the most critical of the three (§10.5, p. 246; §11 item 7(d)). The report describes the change as "compounding an already critical condition" (§10.5, p. 246). However, under the original rod arrangement the connections would have had capacity for the loads estimated at the time of collapse (§11 item 8, p. 256).
12. **Not workmanship or materials.** NBS found no evidence that quality of workmanship or materials "played any significant role" (§10.6, pp. 246 to 248; §11 item 9, p. 256). I found no passage in BSS 143 that blames named people or firms (a search for "negligen", "blame", "fault" and "liab" found only references to court orders and legal counsel). NBS's stated objective was to determine the most probable cause (§1.2, p. 2), and it obtained debris specimens under court orders (Executive Summary p. v).

**Later primary record: Missouri**

13. **The Administrative Hearing Commission decision itself: not obtained.** Search results say it is Case No. AR840239, dated November 14, 1985, and was issued by Judge James B. Deutsch (these come from search summaries, not from a document I read). The Missouri Court of Appeals describes it as 442 pages after twenty-seven days of hearing: 98 pages of findings of fact (180 numbered findings) and 322 pages of conclusions of law. Whether the full decision is online I did not establish.
14. **What I could read: the Court of Appeals opinion.** *Duncan v. Missouri Board for Architects, Professional Engineers and Land Surveyors*, 744 S.W.2d 524 (Mo. Ct. App. 1988), filed January 26, 1988, docket 52655, via https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1529627/ (text is a scan with possible typos, per the site). Passages I read are the introduction and the statement of facts (about the first quarter of the text). They summarize the Commission's findings:
   - Daniel Duncan was the project engineer under Jack Gillum at Gillum-Colaco/G.C.E.; the Board revoked the certificates of Duncan, Gillum and G.C.E. after the Commission found them subject to discipline.
   - Havens proposed the double-rod system to Duncan "because of certain fabricating problems". One architect contacted Duncan to check that it was structurally sound and, the court concluded, was told it was. The appellants disputed that characterization.
   - The Commission found the final structural drawings sent to the fabricator omitted loads and other information that appeared on Duncan's preliminary sketches. It also found the hanger rods and connections shown on the drawings did not meet the Kansas City Building Code, a finding the appellants did not contest.
   - Havens drew its shop drawings on the assumption that the engineer had designed the connections. Duncan testified he intended the fabricator to design them. The court records the Commission's finding that the adequacy of connection design stays with the structural engineer.
   - Havens' shop drawings were returned to Duncan for review and approval. A G.C.E. technician questioned the change from one rod to two and was told it was "basically the same as the one rod concept". The Commission found, and the appellants did not dispute, that they did not review the shop drawings for code compliance or conformance with the design concept; "Duncan and Gillum approved the shop drawings."
15. **A search of the opinion text** for the lower-case word "telephone" found nothing, and "phone" appeared only inside a case citation (*Warner v. Southwestern Bell Telephone*). So the opinion, in the text I searched, does not describe the architect's contact with Duncan as a phone call; it says only "contacted".
16. **The court treats it as a licensing case.** In the part I read, it says Missouri courts do not distinguish between negligence and gross negligence, and that "gross negligence" in the licensing statute means improper conduct greater in kind or degree. A search summary says the court also held that causation is irrelevant in a disciplinary proceeding; I did not read that passage. I read roughly the first third of the opinion (facts, standard of review, the statutory-vagueness argument), not all of it.
17. **Easy to find?** The Court of Appeals opinion: yes (CourtListener, one search). The 1985 Commission decision: not easily; I did not find it as a downloadable document. A Missouri State Archives finding aid exists (per search results, record group RG998, MS519); I did not open it.

### 3. Does the source support the slate's description and angle?

**Partly supported.**
- The slate's facts are supported by the NBS report: report number and date (with the correction above), the rod change roughly doubling the load, 113 dead and 186 injured (receipts 2, 4, 8).
- **The slate's angle** is "a change in the hanger rods during construction", with knowing that sat with different people. The NBS report supports that a change happened and was reviewed on paper by three parties (receipt 5). But it also contradicts a simple "the change did it" telling: the as-designed connection already failed the code and would have had about 60% of the expected capacity (receipts 8, 11). A script that says "the change killed 113 people" would overstate NBS.
- **Where the knowing sat** is not answered by NBS (no names, no dates, no reasons, no calls; receipts 5 to 7). It is answered, in part, by the 1988 court opinion's summary of the Commission's findings (receipts 14, 15): who proposed the change, what the engineer said, what was and was not reviewed, and an unresolved assumption about who designed the connection. That is a good fit for the channel's premise, but I have it second-hand (the Commission decision is unread) and parts are contested (the architect's conversation; Duncan's testimony about intent).
- **Recommendation:** keep the slot. Source the "who held what" claims to the Missouri findings (ideally the Commission decision), not to NBS, and avoid the phrase "nobody checked": the court records that the shop drawings were stamped and approved, and that the engineers did not review the connection for code compliance.

### 4. Differences from the book

The book does not cover this case. A grep of the chapter 7 and chapter 9 texts for "Hyatt", "walkway" and "Kansas" returned nothing. No differences to report.

### 5. New status

## 8B. Eight Signals: the Breakthrough Listen deep-learning search

### 1. Primary document and how I read it

- **Document:** Peter Xiangyuan Ma, Cherry Ng, Leandro Rizk, Steve Croft, Andrew P. V. Siemion and eleven co-authors, "A deep-learning search for technosignatures of 820 nearby stars", Nature Astronomy 7, 492 to 502 (2023), doi 10.1038/s41550-022-01872-z. Nature Astronomy accepted November 30, 2022 (stated on the arXiv copy).
- **What I read:** arXiv 2301.12670 **v1** (dated 30 Jan 2023; the arXiv API shows no later version), 26 pages, downloaded as PDF and converted with pdftotext. I read the abstract, introduction, ML-model description, results, discussion and methods dataset section in full; I skimmed the mathematical methods and supplementary figures. **I did not open the published Nature Astronomy version**, so wording or numbers could differ there.
- Page numbers below are the arXiv PDF page ("n/26").

### 2. Receipts

1. **Targets.** 820 unique targets observed with the Green Bank Telescope, "over 480 hr of on-sky data" (abstract, p. 1). Methods (p. 11): 1004 cadences from 820 unique targets, observed over 480 hr, about 120 TB analyzed, frequency range 1023 to 1926 MHz; each cadence is six 4.8-minute observations. (Mine: 1004 × 6 × 4.8 min ≈ 482 hr, consistent.)
2. **Search size.** "57 million unique snippets" (p. 2, with an overlapping search offset by half a window); and "115 million snippet windows searched" (p. 7), "approximately 115 million snippets" (p. 9). The paper does not reconcile the two in the text I read; the overlap is the likely reason (mine, inference).
3. **Funnel.** The model returned 2,917,789 "signals of interest" (the paper says this is "likely an over count", p. 7). After discarding frequency bins heavily contaminated by interference and requiring a classification score above 90%, 20,515 remained; visual inspection of those found 8 "promising signals of interest" that show narrow, drifted signals in the three ON-source scans (p. 7).
4. **The eight signals.** Table 1 (p. 8): MLc1 and MLc7 toward HIP 13402; MLc2 HIP 118212; MLc3 and MLc8 HIP 62207; MLc4 and MLc5 HIP 54677; MLc6 HIP 56802. So **eight signals from five stars**. Centre frequencies 1188.5 to 1725.0 MHz; drift rates from −0.44 to +1.11 Hz/s; signal-to-noise 6.5 to 129. The five stars are all within 30 to 90 light-years; spectral types K, G, F and M (p. 8).
5. **Earlier searches.** All five stars had been analyzed in two earlier searches of similar GBT data (refs 3 and 5), which "did not detect anything similar" (p. 8). On page 20 the paper adds that none of its top 8 were identified by the TurboSETI pipeline.
6. **What re-observation found.** The five sources were re-observed on **May 21, 2022** at the Green Bank Telescope with the same set-up. The authors ran the same pipeline, visually inspected all **72** events it returned, and state they "do not detect anything like the 8 signals of interest" (p. 8). They infer that, whatever the signals are, they are not persistent in time; they decline to say whether the 8 are produced by extraterrestrial intelligence, and they encourage further re-observations (p. 8). **The abstract's "for re-observation" and this result are the part the slate lacked.**
7. **What the classifier did on test data.** On synthetic (injected) test signals: AUC 0.9993, precision 0.993, recall 0.985, F1 0.989 (p. 6); under 1% false positives at a 90% threshold (p. 6). Limits the paper states: trained only on injected, not real, signals; only signals lasting the full cadence; detection accuracy falls to roughly 70% at 8 Hz/s drift and about 35% at 10 Hz/s (p. 6); and "it is entirely possible" it missed additional ETI-like signals (p. 8).
8. **No claim of discovery.** Stated aim of the work was to apply a machine-learning technique to find a pattern; the authors say they "do not attempt to make a definite conclusion" about the 8 (p. 8).
9. **Million stars.** I searched the paper for "million", "stars" and "Tarter". "Million" appears only for hits and data: 29 and 37 million hits in earlier searches (p. 2), 57 and 115 million snippets, 2.9 million events, 300 million frequency channels. **The paper never mentions a million stars.** The only star count is 820 targets, plus the earlier survey of 692 stars in a reference title (ref 3). Jill Tarter appears only as the author of ref 2, a 2001 review (reference list, p. 9). The introduction says Breakthrough Listen has searched "thousands of stars and hundreds of galaxies" (p. 1).

### 3. Does the source support the slate's description and angle?

- **Facts:** supported. The slate's 820 stars, about 480 hours and 8 signals are all in the paper (receipts 1, 3, 4), and the slate's gap (what re-observation found) is now filled (receipt 6).
- **Angle and premise:** the slate lists it in a channel about systems that failed because nobody held the knowing. **This case does not fit that premise.** Nothing failed: a search flagged 8 signals, the team re-observed, and the signals did not recur. That is a process working as designed, closer to the Apollo 11 counter-example (8C) than to a failure.
- **Options:** (a) keep it as a second counter-example about how to handle a candidate (re-observe before claiming); or (b) swap it for a sourceable failure that fits the theme of the chapter. The slate's own section 6 lists Columbia (CAIB report, 2003) and Microsoft's Tay as alternates that I have **not** read; I make no claim about them. I cannot recommend a specific replacement from what I read.

## 8C. 1202: Apollo 11 program alarms (a counter-example)

### 1. Primary documents and how I read them

Three different things sit behind the slate's "NASA Apollo Lunar Surface Journal":

1. **The page the slate named is not primary and its URL no longer works.** `nasa.gov/history/alsj/a11/a11.1201-pa.html` now redirects to a generic NASA landing page. The Journal has moved to apollojournals.org. The page "Apollo 11 Program Alarms" (https://apollojournals.org/alsj/a11/a11.1201-pa.html) is a **1998 email recollection by Peter Adler**, a software engineer at the MIT Instrumentation Laboratory, "used with permission". Everything on it is later memory, not a 1969 record.
2. **Apollo 11 Mission Report, MSC-00171, NASA Manned Spacecraft Center, November 1969** (NTRS record 19700008096, also NASA TM X-62633). I downloaded the PDF from https://apollojournals.org/alsj/a11/A11_MissionReport.pdf (359 PDF pages) and read the alarm passages: §4.10.3, §5.3 with Table 5-I, §9.6.5, §13.1, §16.2.5. Text is OCR (see top of file). The report credits no author name for these sections.
3. **ALSJ "The First Lunar Landing" air-to-ground transcript** (https://apollojournals.org/alsj/a11/a11.landing.html), Corrected Transcript and Commentary © 1995 Eric M. Jones. The time-stamped lines are the 1969 air-to-ground conversation; the bracketed notes are editors' and contributors' commentary, including later recollections by Armstrong and Aldrin and quotes from the 1969 crew technical debrief. I read the descent section from about 102:15 to 102:44 and searched the whole page for 1201, 1202, "alarm", Bales and Garman.

**Not found:** the Mission Control Houston loop recording or its transcript (the flight controllers' own channel). The Journal transcript carries the capsule communicator and crew conversation only. I did not look in the Apollo Flight Journal for the descent; its landing-prep pages end at powered descent initiation.

### 2. Receipts

Source tags: **[MR]** Mission Report (1969, primary); **[A2G]** the time-stamped air-to-ground transcript lines (1969, primary); **[ALSJ-note]** editors' commentary or quoted later recollection on the same page; **[Adler 1998]** the recollection page.

1. **What the alarms were.** Five computer program alarms during powered descent: the first came 5 minutes 16 seconds after ignition and they continued intermittently for more than 4 minutes (§4.10.3, PDF p. 37) **[MR]**. §16.2.5 (PDF p. 291): "Executive overflow" alarms, meaning the guidance computer could not do all the processing requested in one computation cycle **[MR]**.
2. **Alarm log.** Table 5-I (PDF p. 62) lists: 102:38:22 "1202 alarm (computer determined)"; 102:39:02 1202; 102:42:18 "1201 alarm (computer determined)"; 102:42:43 1202; 102:42:58 1202 (descent engine ignition was at 102:33:05) **[MR]**. **To check against the page image:** an ALSJ note says Table 5-I gives the 1201 as 102:41:32 and the 1202 as 102:39:02; my OCR of the table shows 102:41:32 as "Enter program P64" and 102:42:18 as the 1201. The ALSJ note may be citing a different row or version.
3. **Cause, as the primary document states it.** More than 10 percent of the computer's capacity was taken by unexpected counter interrupts of the kind generated by the coupling data units that serve the **rendezvous radar** shaft and trunnion resolvers; the Executive program then scheduled the same jobs repeatedly until it ran out of storage and raised the alarm (§16.2.5, PDF pp. 291 to 292) **[MR]**. The report attributes the interrupts to the configuration of the interface between the rendezvous radar, its coupling data units and the computer: when the radar mode switch is not in the LGC position, the radar's 28 V, 800 Hz excitation comes from a source that is locked in frequency but not in phase with the guidance system's, so the data units "slew" and send up to 6.4 kpps (12.8 kpps for two units) of pulses; at the maximum rate that is 15 percent of computer time (PDF pp. 292 to 293) **[MR]**. **The report does not say the crew made an error**; it calls the alarms "unexpected" (§9.6.5, PDF p. 164) and the counter interrupts "meaningless" (§16.2.5, PDF p. 293) **[MR]**.
4. **The "misconfigured switches" story is later recollection.** Adler says repeated jobs to process rendezvous radar data "that of course were not really there" were scheduled because of "a misconfiguration of the radar switches" **[Adler 1998]**; he frames it as his own recollection. The Mission Report supports a radar-interface configuration cause but not a crew mistake. Armstrong, in the 1969 technical debrief quoted by ALSJ, recalls only a separate 500-series alarm before ignition that cleared when he cycled the antenna, and says "Certainly the switches were in the right positions." **[ALSJ-note quoting the 1969 debrief]**.
5. **Who decided to continue, and on what basis.** Time-stamped 1969 lines: at 102:38:53 CapCom Charlie Duke says "We're Go on that alarm"; at 102:42:25, on the 1201, "We're Go. Same type." **[A2G]**. The Mission Report §13.1 (PDF p. 269) says the overloading of the guidance computer "was accurately assessed" and that the information given to the crew permitted continuing the descent **[MR]**.
6. **The pre-prepared basis, per the Mission Report.** It "has been decided before flight" that bailout-type alarms like these would not prevent continuing the flight, even though they could cause violations of other mission rules such as velocity differences. Because the alarms were not continuous and navigation was proper, "a decision was given to continue the descent" (§13.1, PDF p. 269) **[MR]**. The report names no flight controller. A search of the whole report text for "Bales", "Garman" and "Kranz" found no hit.
7. **The named controller is from commentary only.** The ALSJ bracket after 102:38:59 says that "In Houston, Steve Bales, the control room's expert in the LM guidance systems" had determined that the overflow would not jeopardize the landing, because the computer "will ignore it while it does more important computations" **[ALSJ-note]**. That is an editor's statement on a transcript page, not a 1969 record, and the MIT-testing explanation attached to it is [Adler 1998] again.
8. **What the software did.** Per Adler, on each 1201 or 1202 the computer rebooted, restarted important tasks (steering the descent engine, the crew display), and did not restart the erroneously scheduled radar jobs; "The NASA guys in the MOCR knew" the restart had been extensively tested by MIT **[Adler 1998]**. The Mission Report states the executive overflow alarms are "now known to be normal for the existing situation" and "indicative of proper performance" of the computer (§9.6.5, PDF p. 164) **[MR]**.
9. **What the crew knew.** An editor's note says the 1202 was not an alarm the crew had seen in simulations, and quotes Armstrong from a post-flight press conference saying that in simulations they were "spring-loaded to the abort position" but in real flight to land **[ALSJ-note]**. Aldrin, recalling later, says he "was not made aware" of what the flight directors knew about alarm potential, "a flaw in communications" **[ALSJ-note, later recollection]**. Another note, citing Kranz's book, says the final pre-mission simulation was aborted by Mission Control for a 1201 alarm **[ALSJ-note, second-hand]**.
10. **Effect on the landing.** The Mission Report says the alarms did not degrade any primary guidance or control function, but "did interfere" with the crew's early assessment of the approach (§5.3, PDF p. 53) **[MR]**. Armstrong's 1969 debrief says their attention went to clearing the alarms and keeping the computer flying rather than studying the landing area **[ALSJ-note quoting the 1969 debrief]**.
11. **Fix.** Section 16.2.5 closes the item: later missions' program (Luminary 1B) will not process the meaningless radar interrupts, and the data units are zeroed when the radar is not powered up or the switch is not in LGC (PDF p. 293) **[MR]**. §13.1 records two checklist changes after the surface stay, including keeping the rendezvous radar off during ascent firing "to prevent computer overload" **[MR]**.
12. **Checks for a script.** §13.1 says the descent team ran simulations "with the Apollo 12 crew" (OCR; confirm on the page image).

### 3. Does the source support the slate's description and angle?

**Partly supported, with a twist the episode must handle.**
- Supported: a counter-example in which the people on the ground had a response ready. The 1969 report says the continue-descent rule was decided before flight (receipt 6), and the transcript shows the "Go" calls in real time (receipt 5).
- Not supported as stated: the slate says a controller "recognized the alarm from a simulation". In the primary documents I read, the report names no controller and no simulation. The controller (Bales) and the simulation story appear only in later commentary (receipts 7 and 9), and the 1998 recollection supplies the "MIT tested the restart" explanation.
- The twist: the **cause** was not known beforehand (the Mission Report calls the alarms "unexpected" and the interrupts a configuration side-effect), and **the crew did not hold the knowing**: they had not seen the alarm in simulation and, per Aldrin's recollection, were not told what the ground's rules were (receipt 9). What was held, by Mission Control and the software designers, was the general rule and the software's recovery design. That is a better, more honest episode than "the knowing was held" without qualification: the knowing was held in one place and not another, and the system worked anyway. It still fits the channel as a counter-example.

### 4. Differences from the book's telling

The book's Apollo passage (chapter 8, opening) does not mention the program alarms, 1201, 1202 or any controller; the grep of the whole chapter for "alarm" and "1202" found nothing. It says the Apollo Guidance Computer "possessed a mere 4KB of RAM" and that astronauts navigated "240,000 miles". The 4KB figure is not one the Mission Report passages I read give; Adler's recollection gives 2,048 words of erasable memory and 36,864 words of fixed memory, 15-bit words (mine: 2,048 × 15 bits ≈ 3.75 KB, about 4 KB with a parity bit; I have not verified parity from a primary source). So the book neither contradicts nor covers this episode; 8C is new material relative to the book.

### 5. New status

**READ** for the Mission Report passages and the air-to-ground transcript listed above. The slot's named-controller claim stays **unsupported by any primary document I read** (it rests on ALSJ commentary). The Journal's "Program Alarms" page is a recollection, not a primary source.

## 9A. The Programme: UK National Programme for IT in the NHS

### 1. Primary documents and how I read them

1. **National Audit Office, "The National Programme for IT in the NHS: an update on the delivery of detailed care records systems"**, Report by the Comptroller and Auditor General, HC 888, Session 2010 to 2012, **18 May 2011** (signed May 16, 2011). https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2011/05/1012888.pdf, 46 pages. I read the full Summary (printed pp. 4 to 15, paragraphs 1 to 32, Figures 1 to 5) and the passages on roles and governance (§1.8; Part Five §5.8). I did not read Parts Two to Four in detail.
2. **NAO memorandum for the Committee of Public Accounts, "Review of the final benefits statement for programmes previously managed under the National Programme for IT in the NHS"**, June 2013. https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/10171-001_NPfiT_Review.pdf, 24 pages. I read the Overview and "The National Programme for IT" (printed pp. 4 to 9), "The benefits statement" (paras 18 to 21) and "Governance arrangements" (paras 22 to 24). I did not read the remaining findings or appendix.
3. **House of Commons Committee of Public Accounts, Nineteenth Report of Session 2013-14, "The dismantled National Programme for IT in the NHS", HC 294**, published 18 September 2013 (page footer: "Prepared 18 September 2013"; ordered to be printed 15 July 2013). https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm201314/cmselect/cmpubacc/294/ (HTML sections 29402 to 29406). Read in full: Summary, Conclusions and recommendations, and chapters 1 and 2 with their footnotes. **Not read:** the formal minutes and the oral and written evidence (the hearing was June 12, 2013; witnesses Sir David Nicholson, Charlie Massey and Tim Donohoe). The NAO site URL `publications.parliament.uk/.../294.pdf` was blocked to curl; I read the HTML through the built-in browser.

### 2. Receipts

1. **Scale.** The NAO called it an "£11.4 billion programme of investment", launched in 2002 (HC 888, inside cover; Summary ¶1). Spend to 31 March 2011: £6.4 billion (¶1; Figure 3 shows £6.35bn actual and £5.05bn remaining to 2015-16).
2. **Spent on care records systems.** £1.8 billion paid to suppliers plus £0.9 billion spent by local NHS organisations implementing the systems, i.e. "£2.7 billion" (HC 888 ¶7 and ¶16; Figure 3: 1.78 and 0.88). In 2003 to 2004 five ten-year contracts, about £5 billion in total, were awarded to four suppliers (Accenture, BT, CSC, Fujitsu) (¶4).
3. **What was delivered.** National systems "almost complete" and providing valuable infrastructure and services (¶2: £2 billion). For detailed care records, Figure 5 (printed p. 11): as at 31 March 2011, 19 acute trusts delivered and 103 remaining (84%) across London, South, and North, Midlands and East; in the North, Midlands and East, 4 acute trusts delivered against 93 remaining (96%), and no mental health trust had received a system. Paragraph 18 gives a different count for the same region: only 10 of 97 acute-trust systems delivered. The report does not reconcile the 4 in Figure 5 with the 10 in ¶18 in the pages I read, and I did not resolve it (flag for a human).
4. **The original aim and the missed date.** The aim was an electronic care record for every NHS patient by 2010 (¶16). The report says "the aim of creating an electronic record for every NHS patient will not be achieved under the Programme" (¶11). Scope was cut (e.g. acute-trust systems down 29%, GP practice systems down 22% overall) without a matching cost cut: in London just £73 million saved out of £1,021 million (¶13; Figure 4).
5. **The NAO's conclusion on value for money (¶27, printed p. 13).** Progress "has fallen far below expectations" and the Department has not delivered care records systems "with anywhere near the completeness of functionality" to achieve the original aspirations. So "the £2.7 billion spent on care records systems so far does not represent value for money", and it found no grounds for confidence that the remaining planned £4.3 billion would differ. The Department's reply (¶28) is that the money "has not been wasted" and will potentially deliver value, since more than half of trusts had received systems and no supplier is paid until a trust verifies deployment.
6. **Unreconciled information.** The NAO says its findings are presented against "a lack of clarity" between the Department and its suppliers about basic management information, and that figures from the suppliers on 13 May did not reconcile with the Department's from the day before; the Department could not explain the difference by the time the report went to be published (¶9). The Department could not give a breakdown of what it had paid for each system (¶16) or a full breakdown of the cost effect of scope changes (¶13).
7. **Warnings repeated.** Figure 1 (p. 5) lists NAO and Committee reports from 2006, 2007, 2008, 2009 and 2010 recording delays; ¶29 says "Despite repeated warning signs" the problems persisted and the Department "has now compromised the vision of the fully integrated care record system".
8. **Who was accountable.** HC 888 §1.8 and the 2013 memo ¶16: the programme was run nationally by NHS Connecting for Health, part of the Department of Health's Informatics Directorate, with **the Chief Executive of the NHS as Senior Responsible Owner**. The Department procured and managed the central contracts; delivery responsibility was split between the local service providers and NHS trusts, which were responsible for business change, delivery plans, staff training and signing off acceptance of systems. HC 888 ¶34 and §5.8 add that the NHS reorganisation would abolish strategic health authorities by 2012, and that it was "not yet known" who would manage the contracts to 2016 or report on benefits.
9. **Governance after dismantling.** The government announced in September 2011 that it would dismantle the Programme into separate components (HC 294 Conclusion 1; memo ¶2). The 2013 memo says each component now has its own senior responsible owner, and it found "strong corporate memory and continuity" through retained officials (memo ¶23).
10. **What the 2013 PAC report says happened to the programme.** The Summary says that although the programme has been dismantled it "in effect remains in place" as separate component programmes that continue to incur significant costs. Chapter 1 ¶4: the component programmes are all continuing, existing contracts are being honoured, and the only change the Department could tell the Committee about was new governance arrangements. I did not find the phrase "officially dismantled" (which the slate puts in quotation marks) in the Summary, Conclusions, or chapters 1 and 2.
11. **Money in 2013.** Benefits statement: costs £7.3 billion to March 2012 and a forecast £9.8 billion to end of life (HC 294 ¶5; memo Figure 1, 2004-05 prices). Benefits £3.7 billion to March 2012 ("just half" of costs), forecast £10.7 billion, two-thirds still to come; for three programmes, 98 percent of benefits were still future (HC 294 ¶¶12 to 13; Conclusion 4: "extremely disappointing"). **Do not set the £11.4bn of 2011 against the £9.8bn of 2013 as a fall**: the 2013 forecast leaves out future costs of the CSC Lorenzo contract and possible Fujitsu arbitration costs (HC 294 ¶¶5 and 11; memo ¶8), and I did not check that the two totals use the same basis.
12. **The CSC contract.** Originally worth £3.1 billion (signed 2003) for 220 trusts; a decade later "not a single trust has a fully functioning Lorenzo" system (HC 294 ¶6). The Department's negotiating position was weak because it could not supply the 160 trusts it was contractually bound to make available (¶8); it paid CSC £100 million compensation for removing exclusivity (¶9); the likely cost was about £2.2 billion, with £572 million for Lorenzo assuming 22 trusts (Conclusion 2; ¶10). Fujitsu's contract had been terminated in 2008; arbitration was ongoing and the Department's legal costs were £31.5 million (¶11).
13. **The PAC's own diagnosis.** Conclusion 6: the Committee's earlier reports found poor negotiating capability, weak programme management and oversight, "failures to understand the complexity of the tasks", failure to recognise the difficulty of persuading trusts to take nationally procured systems, and failure to get people to operate them effectively. Also (Conclusion 4, ¶14) the Department "acknowledged that insufficient attention has been paid to securing benefits".
14. **No baseline.** The NAO memo ¶7 and ¶21: it is not possible to compare the forecast benefits with what was expected at the outset because the Department "did not establish a comprehensive baseline".

### 3. Does the source support the slate's description and angle?

**Partly supported.**
- Slate facts supported: £2.7 billion on care records, judged not value for money (receipt 5); Sept 18, 2013 PAC report on the programme as dismantled but continuing (receipt 10).
- The slate gives no explicit "where the knowing sat" angle for 9A, so I tested the likely one. The best-supported "knowing" strand is that the Department and its suppliers could not give reconciled basic information (receipt 6), could not say what it had bought per system (receipts 2, 6), set no benefits baseline (receipt 14), and was repeatedly warned (receipt 7). The PAC's own diagnosis (receipt 13) is closer to "failures to understand complexity" and weak commercial management than to "nobody held accountability": the Chief Executive of the NHS was the named Senior Responsible Owner (receipt 8), and the NAO found continuity of officials after the break-up (receipt 9).
- If the episode wants "nobody held the knowing", the honest version is narrower: management information and benefits baselines were missing or unreconciled, and ownership of delivery was split between the centre, suppliers and trusts. A broader claim about meaning or context would go beyond these three documents.

### 4. Does this match chapter 9's framing?

**No, not closely.** The book's chapter 9 healthcare IT passage (chapter-09.md, "Example: Healthcare IT") says each hospital has a sophisticated electronic health record, the systems do not coordinate or share data across hospitals, and the result is "fragmented care, repeated tests, medical errors". The NAO and PAC documents describe the opposite starting point: a centrally procured national programme to build common care records that stalled over delivery, contracts and local uptake. The one overlap is the NAO's warning that moving to locally led delivery and a variety of existing systems raises the risk of not achieving "adequate compatibility" across the NHS, with interoperability costing "at least £220 million" (HC 888 ¶12). The reports do not describe errors, repeated tests or harm to patients from fragmentation; HC 888 ¶8 says it does not try to quantify benefits or staff views. So this case would illustrate a different failure than the book's passage.

### 5. New status

**READ** for the three documents' sections listed in section 1. Reason: I read the NAO summary and conclusion, the 2013 memo overview and governance parts, and the whole Committee report text; oral evidence and the rest of the NAO reports are unread.

## 9B. The Score: Epic Sepsis Model external validation

### 1. Primary document and how I read it

- **Document:** Andrew Wong, Erkin Otles, John P. Donnelly, Andrew Krumm, Jeffrey McCullough, Olivia DeTroyer-Cooley, Justin Pestrue, Marie Phillips, Judy Konye, Carleen Penoza, Muhammad Ghous, Karandeep Singh, "External Validation of a Widely Implemented Proprietary Sepsis Prediction Model in Hospitalized Patients", JAMA Internal Medicine 181(8):1065 to 1070, published online June 21, 2021; doi 10.1001/jamainternmed.2021.2626. **Corrected August 2, 2021** (the number needed to evaluate in Table 2 and the Results); I read the corrected text.
- **How:** the JAMA page was blocked (bot challenge, in curl and in the built-in browser). I downloaded the PubMed Central page https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8218233/ (NIH author manuscript with full text, tables and the correction note) and read the whole article text: abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, limitations, conclusions. I did **not** read the online Supplement (eMethods and eFigures 1 to 3). I did not read the accompanying JAMA editorial (Habib, Lin, Grant, "The Epic Sepsis Model Falls Short", doi 10.1001/jamainternmed.2021.3333), which I know of from the PMC page. There are no page numbers in the PMC text, so locators are section names and table numbers.

### 2. Receipts

1. **Cohort.** Retrospective cohort at Michigan Medicine (University of Michigan, Ann Arbor): 27,697 patients, 38,455 hospitalizations, December 6, 2018 to October 20, 2019; 21,904 women (57%), median age 56. Sepsis occurred in 2,552 hospitalizations (7%). (Abstract; Results; Table 1.)
2. **Single site.** Limitations: "a single academic medical center", though the cohort was large and relatively diverse. (Limitations.)
3. **How sepsis was defined.** Composite of the CDC clinical surveillance definition or an ICD-10 sepsis diagnosis plus two SIRS criteria and one CMS organ-dysfunction criterion within 6 hours of one another. (Methods, "Definition of Sepsis and Timing of Onset".)
4. **Discrimination.** Hospitalization-level AUC **0.63** (95% CI 0.62 to 0.64). Time-horizon AUCs (4 to 24 hours) were 0.72 to 0.76, which the authors call "misleading" because each prediction is treated as independent. (Results; Table 2; Discussion.)
5. **At the alert threshold of ESM score 6 or higher** (hospitalization level): sensitivity **33%**, specificity **83%**, positive predictive value **12%**, negative predictive value 95%. Median lead time between first reaching 6 and sepsis onset: 2.5 hours (interquartile range 0.5 to 15.6). Calibration "poor at all time horizons" (Results, "ESM Performance"; Figure 1).
6. **Alert burden.** A score of 6 or higher occurred in **18%** of hospitalizations (6,971 of 38,455). The model did not identify 1,709 of the 2,552 sepsis patients (**67%**), of whom 1,030 (60%) still got timely antibiotics. It identified 183 (7%) septic patients who did not receive timely antibiotics. Number needed to evaluate: 8 patients per true sepsis case if each patient is flagged once; 109 at a 4-hour horizon if re-evaluated each time the score is 6 or higher (Results; Table 2, the corrected figures).
7. **The threshold.** Score 6 was chosen by Michigan's hospital operations committee and was in use at Michigan Medicine for paging clinicians; it is within the developer's recommended range of 5 to 8. Alerts had been live on two units from March 11, 2019 and a third from August 12, 2019; alert-eligible hospitalizations were excluded. No actual alerts were generated in the analyzed patients ("no actual alerts were generated", Figure 2 caption). (Methods; Figure 2.)
8. **How the vendor's own documentation compares, as the paper states it.** The model was developed and validated by Epic on 405,000 patient encounters across 3 health systems from 2013 to 2015. The authors' hospitalization-level AUC of 0.63 was "substantially worse" than Epic's AUC of **0.76 to 0.83** in internal documentation "shared with permission" and than 0.73 in a prior conference proceeding coauthored with Epic. They attribute the gap mainly to how sepsis timing is handled: counting scores up to 3 hours after onset raised their AUC to 0.80. (Introduction; Discussion; Sensitivity Analysis.)
9. **What the authors say about secrecy and independent checks.** Because the model is proprietary, "only limited information is publicly available" and "no independent validations have been published to date, to our knowledge". They say proprietary model performance documents are confidential, not peer reviewed, and may not reflect real-world performance, and that hundreds of US hospitals have adopted such tools through ease of EHR integration and "loose federal regulations". (Introduction; Conclusions.)
10. **Conclusion.** The ESM "has poor discrimination and calibration in predicting the onset of sepsis"; its adoption despite poor performance "raises fundamental concerns about sepsis management on a national level". (Abstract; Discussion.)
11. **Stated limitations.** Two, both in the Limitations paragraph: single academic medical centre; a composite sepsis definition, with sepsis definitions "still debated". No patient-outcome analysis; the study is retrospective and does not test the model in live use. (The last two points are mine, from the Methods and Figure 2 caption.)
12. **Funding and conflicts.** NIH grants supported two authors; the funders "had no role" in design, analysis or the decision to publish. I did not read the full conflict-of-interest paragraph beyond its start. (Back matter.)

**Epic's response.** I could not find Epic's own published response, in the places I searched (one web search for an epic.com, epicshare.org or Epic Research statement; none turned up). What I found is press relay: an HCI Innovation Group article that I fetched and read says an Epic spokesperson, quoted in a Wired report, disputed the conclusions, said the system "helped clinicians save thousands of lives", pointed to a separate Prisma Health study of about 11,500 patients that associated the system with a 4 percent mortality reduction, and said the Michigan study used a low alert threshold that would raise false positives. Search-result summaries of coverage in Fierce Healthcare and Healthcare IT News (both blocked my fetch, so I did not read them) say Epic added that the authors used a hypothetical approach that ignored tuning needed before deployment, and that the model's formula and inputs are available to customers' administrators. These are second-hand; I did not verify any of Epic's claims or the Prisma study.

### 3. Does the source support the slate's description, and does it fit the channel's premise?

- **Slate facts:** supported. The slate says 38,455 hospitalizations and AUC 0.63 (receipts 1, 4) and that Epic disputed it (press only, above).
- **Fit to "a system deployed without anyone holding the knowledge of how it performed in each hospital": partly.**
  - For: the paper says the model is used at hundreds of hospitals and no independent validation had been published (receipts 8, 9); one hospital's own check came out well below the vendor's internal figures (receipt 8).
  - Against: (a) the paper validates **one** hospital, so it cannot show how the model performed "in each hospital" (receipt 2); (b) the vendor did hold performance figures, and the authors saw them (receipt 8), so "nobody held" it is wrong; the more accurate claim is that the vendor held numbers nobody outside could check; (c) the hospital in question (Michigan) chose its own alert threshold and was running live alerts on three units (receipt 7), and did this validation itself, so at least one hospital held local knowledge (the paper does not say whether it validated before it turned alerts on); (d) the paper does not show harm to any patient (receipt 11).
  - **Better framing from the source:** a vendor's own performance claims, behind a proprietary wall, widely deployed with no independent check until a university team did one; the gap between the vendor's and the hospital's numbers traces mostly to how timing was handled (receipt 8). That is a "claims not independently checkable" story, not strictly a "nobody held the knowing" story.
- **Relation to the book's Stanford claim.** The book's chapter 9 opener says radiologists at Stanford in 2016 tested an AI system that diagnosed pneumonia with 95% accuracy; "The hospital deployed it. Six months later, they shut it down", with a list of workflow, liability and insurance failures. The slate says it could not source that. The Wong et al. paper is a different case and a different failure mode (external validity of a vendor model, not workflow), so it replaces the book's opener only as an episode slot, not as a check of the book's claim.

### 4. Differences from the book's telling

Chapter 9 does not mention Epic, sepsis, or this study (grep of chapters 7 and 9 for "sepsis" and "Epic " returned nothing). No differences to report beyond receipt in section 3.

### 5. New status

**READ** (PMC author manuscript of the JAMA article; JAMA page and supplement not opened). Epic's side remains **UNVERIFIED** (press relay only). Reason: the numbers, the vendor comparison and the limitations are read from the full paper text.
