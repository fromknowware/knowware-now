## 7A replacement: Columbia accident, February 1, 2003

### 1. Document and how I read it

*Report of the Columbia Accident Investigation Board*, Volume I, August 2003. Downloaded from `https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/caib-report.pdf` (a 3,535-page file that begins with Volume I; I used the first 260 pages) and converted with `pdftotext`. Printed page = PDF page minus 1. **Read:** Chapter 6 "Decision Making at NASA", its opening (pp. 121 to 122), the foam-history conclusion (p. 130), the STS-107 imagery requests (pp. 150 to 151), the "Summary" and Findings F6.3-1 to F6.3-29 (pp. 170 to 172). **Not read:** Chapters 5, 7 and 8 (the organizational and historical analysis, including the Board's comparison with Challenger), sections 6.2 and 6.4 beyond their findings headings, and Volumes II to VI.

### 2. Receipts

1. **Loss.** On February 1, 2003 Columbia was destroyed in a disaster that claimed the lives of all seven of its crew (front matter, printed p. 6).
2. **A requirement that was not met (p. 122).** The shuttle's design requirements said the External Tank was to shed no debris that would jeopardize the crew or vehicle, and the Orbiter's thermal protection was to withstand impacts below 0.006 foot-pounds of energy. Columbia was damaged by debris on its first flight in 1981; more than 300 tiles were replaced.
3. **Foam loss was normal (pp. 122 and 130).** Photographic evidence shows foam shedding on 65 of the 79 missions with imagery available. Over 113 missions, foam-shedding and other debris impacts "came to be regarded more as a turnaround or maintenance issue", not a hazard to vehicle and crew (p. 130).
4. **Words and meaning (p. 130).** The Board says program managers appear to have confused foam as an "accepted risk" with foam not being a "safety-of-flight issue"; assessments were not substantiated by engineering analysis; and at times schedule pressure appeared to cut short engineering efforts.
5. **The strike was noticed on Flight Day Two (F6.3-1 to F6.3-3, p. 170).** The Intercenter Photo Working Group saw it during standard review of launch video; the strike was larger than any seen before; no conclusive images existed (one camera that might have helped was out of focus because of a lens not properly maintained). The group's chair asked management to begin getting outside imagery: the first of three requests.
6. **Initial estimates were accurate (F6.3-4, p. 171).** The first report, widely distributed, correctly said the debris came from the left bipod area, was large and fast, and struck the underside of the left wing near the leading edge; it could have hit tile or the reinforced carbon-carbon (RCC) panels.
7. **The Debris Assessment Team's request took the wrong route (F6.3-5 to F6.3-9, p. 171).** The Team asked for imagery through the Johnson Space Center Engineering Directorate rather than the mission chain (Mission Evaluation Room, Mission Management Team, Flight Dynamics Officer). The Board says this routing "diluted the urgency"; managers viewed it as a noncritical engineering desire. Team members never realized management's decision against imagery was not meant as a final answer to them. No mission manager appeared to "own" the Team.
8. **What the engineers wrote (p. 151).** Co-chair Rodney Rocha's January 21, 2003 email said that without better images it would be very difficult to bound the problem, with analysis results possibly "ranging from acceptable to not-acceptable to horrible".
9. **The analysis was thin, and its limits did not travel (F6.3-10 to F6.3-14, p. 171).** The tile assessment used an impact simulation well outside the test database, by an analyst inexperienced with the tool; experts at Huntington Beach were not actively involved; RCC conclusions rested mainly on judgment and experience. The assumptions and their uncertainties were never presented in full to the Mission Evaluation Room or Mission Management Team, and briefings did not address RCC damage; neither group asked about it.
10. **Management's stance (F6.3-15 to F6.3-23, pp. 171 to 172).** Managers entered the mission believing a foam strike is not a safety-of-flight issue, reinforced by the earlier STS-113 Flight Readiness Review. Mission Management Team meetings occurred five times in a 16-day mission, not daily as specified. A "trusted expert" who spoke from "experience" confirmed early, without analysis, that tile damage would be a turnaround issue; no one questioned this (and pp. 150 to 151 add there was no discussion of RCC damage). Managers asked "Who's requesting the photos?" instead of assessing the request. Engineers had to produce evidence the system was unsafe, not prove it safe.
11. **Safety (F6.3-29, p. 172).** Safety representatives attended the Debris Assessment Team, Mission Evaluation Room and Mission Management Team meetings but were passive and not a channel for concerns or dissent.
12. **The Board's summary (p. 170).** Management decisions reflect "missed opportunities, blocked or ineffective communications channels, flawed analysis, and ineffective leadership"; managers "displayed no interest in understanding a problem and its implications". The Board adds that people's actions are shaped by their organizations, so replacing the people involved would not by itself solve the problems.

### 3. Fit with the channel's premise

**Strong.** The knowing existed: a photo group saw a large strike on day two, engineers correctly estimated its origin and wrote that uncertainty could not be reduced without images, and the request was made three separate ways. It did not reach the deciders as a decision-relevant request, because of routing, informal channels, a trusted expert's reassurance and a question about who was asking. The Board's own text gives the angle.

**Cautions for the owner.**
- **Overlap with 3A Challenger.** Both are NASA cases about knowledge that did not reach the decision. The Board draws this link in Chapter 8 ("History as Cause: Columbia and Challenger"), which I did not read. If both stay on the slate, give them different angles or pick one.
- **Chapter 7.** The slate placed 7A in "Engineering Reality", a chapter with no failure; Columbia is not in the book, like most additions.
- This replaces Amazon (7A), which had no primary source.

## 5B replacement: the Post Office Horizon judgment, December 16, 2019

### 1. Document and how I read it

*Bates and Others v Post Office Limited (No 6: Horizon Issues)* [2019] EWHC 3408 (QB), High Court, Queen's Bench Division, Mr Justice Fraser, December 16, 2019, 313 pages. Downloaded from `https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/bates-v-post-office-judgment.pdf`, converted with `pdftotext`. Cited below by paragraph number. **Read:** paragraphs 100 to 111 (a witness's evidence), 661 to 663 (the experts' joint statement on Issue 1), 925 to 935, and 941 to 945 (the overall conclusions on the evidence). **Not read:** the rest of the judgment, including the Technical Appendix with the findings on each Horizon Issue, and the other judgments in the litigation (such as Judgment No. 3 on Common Issues); I did not read anything about prosecutions or later events.

### 2. Receipts

1. **What the judgment is.** The Horizon Issues trial: a ruling on whether the Post Office's Horizon accounting system (in its "Legacy" and "Horizon Online" forms) could cause apparent shortfalls in sub-postmasters' accounts, brought by former and serving sub-postmasters against the Post Office (¶¶ 1 and 661 to 663).
2. **What the two experts agreed (¶ 663, quoting the first Joint Statement).** Evidence exists that bugs, errors or defects have caused actual discrepancies or shortfalls in sub-postmasters' accounts; any change to an IT system can introduce new bugs; discovered bugs take time to resolve, so systems like Horizon often keep running with them, with or without workarounds. They disagreed on the extent of the impact.
3. **Where they differed (¶ 663).** The claimants' expert, Jason Coyne, pointed to many thousands of known errors and examples including the Callendar Square/Falkirk bug, the Payments Mismatch defect (at least 62 branches; "not capable of being identified by Subpostmasters") and the Suspense Account bug (14 branches), and said Horizon had at least 19,842 software releases. The Post Office's expert, Dr Worden, took a "top down" statistical approach and said undetected bugs were very unlikely to cause permanent shortfalls; the total claimed shortfall he cited was of the order of £18 million.
4. **The Post Office could no longer deny the bugs (¶ 925).** By the end of the trial the Post Office's own closing summary was "forced to accept the existence of a great many bugs"; the extent and impact were not agreed. The judge cites a Fujitsu employee's February 2006 entry that a bug "has been around for years" as one example of terms showing these were known to be bugs.
5. **The system's chief architect (¶ 927).** The evidence of Mr Godeseth, Horizon's Chief Architect and a main Post Office witness, was "sufficiently damaging" to the Post Office's case that the Post Office was "forced almost to disavow" him. In one example, a trainer trusted by the Post Office (Mr Latif) said £2,000 transferred between terminals "disappeared"; the Post Office answered that if he had followed procedure it could not have happened. The judge calls this answer an assertion that ignores his primary evidence.
6. **The court's characterization (¶¶ 928 to 929).** The judge describes "institutional obstinacy" by the Post Office in refusing to consider alternatives to its view of Horizon, regardless of the evidence, and likens the approach to "the 21st century equivalent of maintaining that the earth is flat".
7. **What was held back (¶ 934).** The judge says Fujitsu had powers over the system (¶ 932, nearby, credits a former Fujitsu witness's evidence with leading to "the correct picture on remote access finally emerging", so the powers are probably remote access, but ¶ 934 itself does not say so) which, until shortly before the trial started, Fujitsu sought to keep from the court, and may not even have fully disclosed to the Post Office; as a result the Post Office had made misleading public statements. He sees "a lack of transparency, and a lack of accuracy in description" and a pattern of defensiveness.
8. **Late disclosure (¶ 941).** Fujitsu disclosed some 5,000 Known Error Log entries only in September 2019, months after the trial ended; the judge says he took no account of them.
9. **What a sub-postmaster was told (¶ 106).** In a 2015 interview a Post Office investigator told Mr Tank "there are no issues with Horizon", while Mr Tank said he had evidence the investigator had not looked at; the judge says on the face of it that was a valid point.
10. **The design intent (¶ 942).** Internal 2009 Post Office training slides say the purpose of the Horizon Online programme was to cut the system's total annual cost of ownership; the judge notes no significant upgrade in functionality was intended.

### 3. Fit with the channel's premise

**Strong, and a different kind of fit from Neuralink.** The knowing about faults was held in Fujitsu's and the Post Office's own records and by their staff (receipts 4, 7, 8). The people using the system and the court did not have it, and the operator's public and investigative stance said there were no issues (receipt 9). The judge says so in terms (receipts 6 and 7), and these are court findings after a trial with experts on both sides, not a press account. The slate's 5B slot is Chapter 5 "Human-Systems Intelligence Interaction"; the fit is people with first-hand experience of a system being contradicted by an institution that held contrary knowledge.

**Cautions.**
- I read only the overall-conclusions part of this one judgment of the litigation. The Technical Appendix, the other judgments, the consequences for sub-postmasters and any later inquiry are not read, so do not script claims about prosecutions or outcomes from this file.
- The judge's characterizations (receipts 5 to 7) are findings on the evidence in this trial; the Post Office disputed many points, and the Court of Appeal and later events are outside what I read.
- The case is not in the book. Dollar or pound totals appear only as quoted by an expert (£18 million, receipt 3); the total number of people affected was not in the pages I read.

### 4. Status

**READ** for the paragraphs listed; most of the 313 pages unread.

## 4B replacement: HealthCare.gov, October 2013

### 1. Document and how I read it

HHS Office of Inspector General, *HealthCare.gov: CMS Management of the Federal Marketplace: An OIG Case Study*, OEI-06-14-00350, February 2016, 92 pages. Downloaded from `https://oig.hhs.gov/oei/reports/oei-06-14-00350.pdf`. The slate's earlier lead (GAO's 2014 report GAO-14-694) **returned an access-denied error to every address I tried, so I did not read it.** Printed page = PDF page minus 8 for the body. **Read:** the foreword and executive summary (pp. i to iii), the key contributing factors summary, Chapter 2 pp. 21, 22 and 25 in full, and short passages from pp. 27, 29, 31 and 32 (including the launch passage on p. 32). **Not read:** Chapters 1, 3 and 4 in full, the glossary, and appendices.

### 2. Receipts

1. **Scope (Foreword, p. i).** The case study covers the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services' (CMS) implementation of HealthCare.gov and the Federal Marketplace from 2010 to the second open enrollment in 2015. OIG interviewed 86 current and former HHS and CMS officials, staff and contractors and reviewed thousands of documents.
2. **The launch (p. 32, Foreword).** On October 1, 2013 HealthCare.gov had 250,000 concurrent users, far more than the planned capacity; outages began within 2 hours. After corrective action, 5.4 million people had selected a plan through the Federal Marketplace by the end of the first open enrollment (Foreword).
3. **OIG's headline (Executive Summary, pp. i to ii).** The site faced a high risk of failure, but HHS and CMS made many missteps. "Most critical" was the absence of clear leadership; others included poor coordination between policy and technical work, "resistance to communicating and heeding warnings of 'bad news'", and reluctance to alter plans.
4. **Eighteen warnings (p. 21).** Between July 2011 and July 2013 CMS received 18 "documented warnings" about the build, each with substantial detail and formally submitted to CMS senior leadership or project managers. They included six reports by a technical advisor in June to October 2012, eleven reports by TurningPoint Global Solutions in 2012 and 2013 (including an April 2013 list of the top ten risks), and a McKinsey review commissioned by the Secretary in early 2013. By January 2013 the most common advice was that the program needed a single lead entity.
5. **The warnings did not travel (p. 22).** The TurningPoint reports went only to CMS technical staff such as the Deputy CIO; McKinsey's recommendations went only to senior officials such as the Administrator. The Deputy CIO, "the chief CMS technician" on the build, said technical staff did not receive or even know of the McKinsey findings.
6. **Leadership by implication (p. 22).** After criticism, CMS assigned its COO in early 2013 to lead the Federal Marketplace, but the assignment was not formally announced and was not backed by clear responsibilities; staff later said "the group sort of knew".
7. **A role nobody owned (p. 22).** A CMS advisor recommended hiring a technical systems integrator; CMS technical leadership perceived that CMS was already doing that job, while the contractor said the lack of a true integrator created extra work outside its contract; few at CMS had experience integrating a project of this size.
8. **Bad news (p. 25).** Despite knowing the project was going poorly, many staff were averse to alerting leaders, partly from a belief they would succeed in the end and partly because they were busy; HHS IT leadership requested more information from CMS and did not receive it. The top CMS security official later testified that CMS leadership "was not properly briefed" about security-testing issues that week.
9. **Staffing came late (p. 25).** CMS added 60 staff from other projects about two months before launch, a 117 percent increase over the 51 already working on it; people involved said it felt too late and embarrassing to "add others into a mess".
10. **Recovery (Executive Summary, p. ii).** CMS and contractors reorganized after launch around a "badgeless" culture and "ruthless prioritization", and recovered the site for high consumer use within 2 months. CMS concurred with OIG's call to keep applying the lessons.

### 3. Fit with the channel's premise

**Strong, with the knowledge documented in an official self-critical record.** The knowing existed in specific documents (receipt 4); it was held by some people and not others (receipt 5); the person closest to the build did not see the review that senior officials had (receipt 5); and staff avoided passing bad news up (receipt 8). The OIG's own wording supports the angle ("resistance to ... heeding warnings", receipt 3). It is a better source than the Sidewalk Labs material, whose primary statements gave an economic reason.

**Cautions.**
- The GAO report named in the slate's lead was not readable by me; the OIG case study is the single source here.
- It is the OIG's account based on interviews and documents, after the fact; CMS "concurred" with the call for continued progress but I did not read CMS's response in full.
- The slate's 4B slot sits in Chapter 4, which is about cities, medical AI and trading; HealthCare.gov is not in the book. Whether it fits the chapter's theme is the owner's call.

### 4. Status

**READ** for the pages listed; Chapters 1, 3 and 4 and GAO's report are not read.
