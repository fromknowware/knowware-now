## 1. Settled: the Rogers Commission and the CAIB on the O-ring criticality history

The open question (`receipts-options.md` §1, receipt 5) was that the CAIB (p. 196) says the joint was "elevated" to Criticality 1, "later demoted" to 1-R, and then "closed out" in the month before launch, while my earlier read of the Rogers Commission said 1R changed to 1 in 1982 and was then waived.

**Sources:** Rogers Commission Volume I, Chapter VI (printed pp. 125 to 128, 142 to 144) and Chapter VII (p. 156), from `history.nasa.gov/rogersrep/v1ch6.htm` and `v1ch7.htm`; CAIB Volume I, Chapter 8, printed p. 196 (read earlier).

**Rogers' sequence, as I read it:**
1. **November 24, 1980 (p. 125):** the joint was classified on the Critical Items List as Criticality 1R (redundant hardware). The same 1980 list text said redundancy of the secondary seal could not be verified above about 40 percent of operating pressure. Arnold Aldrich, asked about the wording, said he would call it Criticality "1".
2. **To November 1982 (p. 125):** it "carried a 1 R classification" through STS-5.
3. **December 17, 1982 (p. 126):** Marshall accepted the secondary O-ring was no longer functional after joint rotation, so "the Criticality 1R had to be changed to Criticality 1."
4. **March 28, 1983 (pp. 127 to 128):** the Criticality 1 requirement was waived by Levels I and II (Weeks signed it).
5. **After that (pp. 128 and 156):** the Commission says the joint "has been categorized as a Criticality 1 item since 1982", yet most Thiokol and Marshall problem-reporting paperwork still listed it as Criticality 1R, and the Problem Assessment System still did so on March 7, 1986, more than five weeks after the accident. The Commission calls this "misrepresentation of criticality" (p. 156).
6. **Closure (pp. 142 to 144):** on December 6, 1985 a Thiokol engineer asked for closure of the O-ring erosion problems; on December 10 Thiokol's project director asked NASA for closure; Marshall entered "contractor closure received" on all referenced problem reports on December 18, 1985, and "problem is considered closed" on January 23, 1986. Mulloy and Wear testified those entries were "in error".

**Verdict (mine, from the two texts):** the CAIB's "closed out in the month before launch" matches Rogers (receipt 6). The CAIB's "elevated to Criticality 1 ... later demoted to 1-R" does **not** match Rogers as a formal reclassification: Rogers says the formal status went 1R to 1 (1982) and stayed 1 under a waiver, while paperwork kept saying 1R. The CAIB's wording is best read as a loose summary of that paperwork mismatch. **For a script, use Rogers' sequence.** I did not read any source that says the status was formally lowered back to 1R.

## 2. HealthCare.gov: GAO-14-694 (a second official source for the 4B swap)

**Document:** GAO, *Healthcare.gov: Ineffective Planning and Oversight Practices Underscore the Need for Improved Contract Management*, GAO-14-694, July 30, 2014. The PDF returned access denied to curl; I read the GAO product page and its accessible text version (`gao.gov/assets/670/665195.txt`) through the browser. I read the "What GAO Found" highlights and the Background paragraph on obligations. **Not read:** the body, findings sections and recommendations in full.

1. **Without effective planning or oversight.** GAO says CMS developed the site "without effective planning or oversight practices" despite risk. Task orders were issued when key technical requirements were unknown, including the number of states and, importantly, the number of potential enrollees. Cost-reimbursement contracts were used. CMS "did not develop a required acquisition strategy" identifying risks and did not use available information such as quality assurance plans to monitor performance.
2. **Cost growth tied to changing requirements and oversight gaps.** From September 2011 to February 2014, obligations for the federally facilitated marketplace system grew from $56 million to more than $209 million; for the data hub, from $30 million to nearly $85 million.
3. **Who could approve work.** GAO says "unclear guidance and inconsistent oversight" left confusion about who had the authority to approve contractor requests to expend funds for additional work.
4. **The readiness review slipped.** CMS moved a key readiness assessment from March to September 2013, weeks before launch, and did not receive required approvals; GAO says the site launched "without verification that it met performance requirements".
5. **Limited accountability for the contractor.** CMS gave written concerns in April and November 2013, moved operations to the contractor's offices in September 2013, but chose not to withhold fee in order to meet the launch date; it declined to pay about $267,000, about 2 percent of $12.5 million in fees paid. A new $91 million contract followed in January 2014.
6. **Scale.** CMS reported obligating $840 million to Healthcare.gov and supporting systems by March 2014 across 62 contracts and task orders; GAO reviewed two task orders and a January 2014 letter contract totaling $369 million.

**Effect on 4B.** GAO and the OIG case study (`receipts-swaps.md`) emphasize different things: GAO is about contract planning, who could approve spending and oversight; the OIG is about leadership, warnings not shared and staff avoiding bad news. Both are consistent with the swap, and together they give two official, independent-of-each-other sources. The "who had authority" point (receipt 3) is an accountability thread the OIG excerpt did not give. Coverage of GAO is the highlights and one background paragraph only.

## 6. Flu Trends (2C): a swap candidate, read but not applied

`receipts-options.md` §3 rejected the 2C reframe and left 2C as the first slot to swap. A candidate with a primary source that fits the premise better is the Long-Term Capital Management episode.

**Document:** *Hedge Funds, Leverage, and the Lessons of Long-Term Capital Management*, Report of the President's Working Group on Financial Markets (Treasury, Federal Reserve, SEC, CFTC), April 1999 (transmitted April 28, 1999), 140 PDF pages, from `home.treasury.gov/system/files/236/hedgfund.pdf`. **Read:** the transmittal letter, the Executive Summary (pp. viii to ix), printed pp. 10 and 13 to 14 in full, and keyword-located sentences at pp. 17 to 18, 25, 30 to 33. **Not read:** the appendices and most of Public Policy Issues and Conclusions.

1. **The headline (pp. viii to ix).** The "principal policy issue" is how to constrain excessive leverage. "Market discipline can break down": LTCM's investors, creditors and counterparties "did not provide an effective check" on its activities. The report adds that even painful lessons "recede from memory with time".
2. **Reputation (p. 10).** The principals had "substantial reputations" in financial markets and in the economic theory of financial markets, and LTCM was prominent partly for that reason and for its large capital.
3. **The final week (p. 13).** By September 21, 1998 Bear Stearns, the prime broker, had required LTCM to collateralize settlement exposures; repo and derivatives counterparties were seeking collateral through daily margining; a default could have come as soon as September 23; on September 22 a core group of four counterparties began exploring alternatives, and on September 23 fourteen firms agreed to a consortium, with the New York Fed providing the facilities.
4. **The price (p. 14).** The consortium put in about $3.6 billion for a 90 percent stake and operational control; the principals' stake fell to 10 percent. The report says the burden of resolving it remained with "the counterparties that had allowed" LTCM to build its positions.
5. **Opaqueness (p. 14).** LTCM "stood out" for opaqueness, low external monitoring and high leverage; it was the most highly leveraged large hedge fund reporting to the CFTC, holding more than $125 billion in assets, nearly four times the next largest.
6. **What counterparties could see (p. 14).** LTCM's own mark-to-market valuations alerted its managers early, but individual counterparties, "partly because there were so many", were not necessarily aware of the depth of its liquidity problems; the balance sheet and income statements given to counterparties were not very informative about its risk profile and concentrations. The report says this opaqueness "is an important part of the LTCM story".
7. **A number only LTCM held (p. 17).** LTCM estimated its top 17 counterparties would have suffered losses of between $3 billion and $5 billion in aggregate, and shared this with the fourteen consortium firms.

**Fit and cautions (mine).**
- **Premise fit: strong.** Each counterparty saw a slice; the firm held the whole picture, and the report says the reports given to counterparties did not show it (receipts 5 to 7).
- **Overlap:** Chapter 2 already carries two automated-trading stories (2A Knight, 2B Flash Crash); LTCM would be a third finance story in the chapter, and the capstone has two more (XA, XC from the FCIC). That is a real balance cost.
- **Source:** a government working-group report that includes the regulators involved; I did not read any independent account or LTCM's side.
- **The book:** a search of all ten chapters for "LTCM", "Long-Term Capital", "Merton" and "Scholes" found no mention of the case.
- **Not applied.** This is a proposal. The slate still has 2C as it was.
