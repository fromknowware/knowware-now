## XA, remainder: AIG (FCIC Final Report, printed pp. 269 to 273 and 344 to 347)

Printed page = PDF page minus 29. I read printed pp. 269 to 273 and 344 to 347 in full as text; years and amounts were dropped by the text layer on these pages and I did not render them, so the order of events is taken from context (the collateral dispute began in July 2007; the investor call in the next paragraph set is December 2007; the auditors' meeting is February 2008). **Not read:** printed pp. 348 to 349.

1. **A risk tool that depended on the counterparty (p. 269).** PwC, auditor to both AIG and Goldman, knew AIG had never before marked these positions to market. Before the Goldman margin call, PwC had concluded that "compensating controls" made up for AIG having no valuation model; among those controls was notice from counterparties that collateral was due. The Commission's gloss: one of AIG's risk management tools was to learn of its own problems from the counterparties who could mark positions and demand collateral.
2. **Disputing every call (p. 269).** The CFO told the Audit Committee that the unit had received margin calls from five counterparties and was disputing every one. Goldman's marks were sharply lower than Merrill's on the same positions.
3. **The makeshift model (p. 270).** In October AIG began adapting a subprime pricing model from Moody's, with generic CDO data bought from JP Morgan; Cassano called it only a "gut check". PwC questioned its relevance (not validated before the earnings release, ignored structural features of the swap contracts, data-quality doubts); AIG did not mention those caveats on the November earnings call, where the CEO said it was highly unlikely AIG would have to make payments.
4. **No one could price it (p. 271).** Cassano wrote that no one seemed to know how to discern a market valuation from the opaque market. By late November AIG and PwC agreed the Moody's-based model was inadequate. A "negative basis adjustment" proposed by the unit's head of models cut the estimated loss sharply; Cassano and others adopted it "with their auditor's knowledge". PwC's concerns, in a meeting without the unit's executives, included that the unit had paid out collateral without the parent company's enterprise risk group being actively involved, and that the unit was managing what the auditors could see.
5. **Two subsidiaries, opposite strategies (p. 272).** AIG's securities-lending subsidiary kept buying mortgage-backed securities while Financial Products had decided to pull back from CDO swaps. PwC said that was a parent-level risk management failure and that disclosures would have changed had the securities-lending exposure been known. Chief Credit Officer Kevin McGinn wrote that all units were apprised regularly of concerns about the housing market and that "others simply chose not to listen" (CEO Sullivan told the Commission the two business models were just different, not a risk management failure).
6. **What investors were told (p. 272).** On the December call Sullivan said the probability of economic loss was "close to zero". Cassano did not disclose the collateral already posted to Goldman, the hundreds of millions posted to others, or the negative basis adjustment; the Commission says investors therefore did not know earnings were overstated, which came out in February.
7. **The auditors' verdict (p. 273).** In February PwC told the board chairman the adjustment had been improper and unsupported and a material weakness in controls over the swap portfolio's valuation and oversight; the Commission notes it is unclear why PwC waited, since it had known of the adjustment in November. PwC said it could finish the audit only if Cassano "did not interfere in the process" (the Commission's quote of PwC's notes). Sullivan told a Commission hearing he had no recollection of his pay figures.
8. **The September rescue (pp. 344 to 347).** The New York Fed's Kevin Coffey wrote in August that AIG was under increasing capital and liquidity pressure and listed six concerns, including the CDS margin calls and rating triggers. The OTS told the New York Fed it was generally comfortable with AIG's liquidity; the New York Fed disagreed. The Commission says AIG's visit to the Fed was an emergency but "should not have been a surprise" (p. 345).

**Effect on the angle.** Receipt 1 is the sharpest "knowing" fact in this report: AIG's own controls relied on learning of exposure from the counterparties. Receipts 3 to 6 show that people inside and around the firm held doubts and caveats (the auditor, the credit officer, the model questions) that were not passed on to investors. This supports the narrowed XA angle, with the caution already given: the unit's CEO, salesman and general counsel did know the collateral terms (`receipts-d-followup.md` §XA). The year digits above are from context, not read.

## XC, remainder: Moody's (FCIC, printed pp. 118 to 121 and 221 to 223)

1. **Why ratings mattered (pp. 118 to 119).** The Commission says issuers needed ratings to approve deals, banks to set capital, repo markets to set terms, some investors could buy only triple-A, and the ratings were written into collateral agreements and rules. Kolchinsky's summary: "the rating agencies were given a blank check".
2. **Models (pp. 118, 120).** Moody's rated mortgage securities with models based in part on a period of strong credit performance; it did not build a model for the layered risks of subprime securities until late in the cycle, after rating nearly (count dropped) subprime securities. Its first subprime model came in the fall of the year the housing market had peaked. The models put little weight on a sharp national price fall; Jay Siegel, a former team managing director, said Moody's position was that there was no national housing bubble.
3. **Judgment on top of the model (p. 121).** Siegel and Roger Stein said ratings were not purely mathematical: a rating committee applied judgment, and the model "has to contemplate events for which there is no data" (Stein).
4. **The agenda (p. 121).** Jerome Fons, a former managing director on a high-level Structured Credit committee, said that the decline in loan quality going into pools was never raised or put on the agenda: "We talked about everything but... the elephant sitting on the table."
5. **The surveillance signal (pp. 221 to 222).** Moody's created an independent surveillance team; in November it saw a rise in early payment defaults on Fremont loans and put deals rated that same calendar year on watch, "never before" done in the team's experience (Nicolas Weill to the CEO and the president of Moody's Investors Service). In July it downgraded a large block of subprime securities in one mass announcement, by an average of four notches (p. 222, years dropped; context is 2006 to 2007).
6. **Known, and not adjusted (p. 223).** In March a Moody's report said CDOs concentrated in subprime securities could face severe downgrades; an internal email five days later described banks furiously clearing warehouses into CDOs. The Commission says Moody's, though aware, kept rating new CDOs on existing assumptions. A former executive, Richard Michalek, explained that Moody's had a very conservative culture and that quickly adopting the wrong methodology was worse than waiting. In July a presentation to the board had slide titles such as "Spotlight on Mortgages: Quality Continues to Erode"; Moody's made no significant change to CDO assumptions until late September.
7. **The suggestion to stop (p. 223).** In mid-September, Eric Kolchinsky learned from a servicer survey that few troubled mortgages were being modified and advised the group managing director to stop rating CDOs until securities downgrades were done; he said she "admonished" him.

**Effect on the angle.** This strengthens XC beyond receipt 2 of the earlier file: the deterioration was known inside Moody's (surveillance, the March report, the board slides), a senior person proposed pausing, and assumptions did not change for months. The years on these pages were dropped by the text layer and I did not render them; I give the sequence only. Culture and motive claims remain contested by Moody's executives (see earlier file).

## 1A. Facebook's own statement: Adam Kramer's post of June 29

Obtained as a Wayback Machine capture of Kramer's Facebook post, `web.archive.org/web/2014/https://www.facebook.com/akramer/posts/10152987150867796`; the page shows a French-language Facebook interface and the capture date is not stated on the page. I read the whole post.

1. Kramer says the research aimed to test the common worry that friends' positive posts make people feel negative or left out, and the concern that friends' negativity might make people avoid Facebook; he says "We didn't clearly state our motivations in the paper."
2. Method, in his words: "very minimally deprioritizing a small percentage of content" in News Feed, based on whether a post contained an emotional word, for about 0.04% of users (1 in 2,500) for one week in early 2012; nobody's posts were "hidden", they did not show up on some loads of the feed and stayed on friends' timelines.
3. He says the effect was the minimum needed to detect statistically: on average one fewer emotional word per thousand words over the following week.
4. He and his coauthors say they are "very sorry for the way the paper described the research and any anxiety it caused"; that the research benefits "may not have justified all of this anxiety"; and that Facebook researchers had been working on improving internal review practices.

**Effect.** The post confirms the removal (not addition) of content and gives Facebook's own account of the review practices as something being improved. It does not mention consent forms or a university review board. For the slate: 1A coverage can now say Facebook's statement is read via an archived copy (not the live post).

## 5C. Facebook in Myanmar: BSR section 3.2

Document: BSR, *Human Rights Impact Assessment: Facebook in Myanmar* (October 2018, published by Facebook November 5, 2018), 62 pages; I read §3.2 "The Internet in Myanmar" (printed pp. 12 to 13). §3.1 and §3.3 not read.

1. For the majority of Myanmar's 20 million internet-connected citizens, BSR writes that "Facebook is the internet" (p. 12); fewer than 1 percent of 50 million people had a smartphone or home internet in 2009; most phones sold come preloaded with Facebook; BSR describes a "crisis of digital literacy".
2. There are about 20 million Facebook users as of January 2018, roughly equal to internet users (pp. 12 to 13).
3. Facebook is used as a main source of information, including by government departments, and BSR says it has had democratizing effects as well as being used to spread rumors, character assassination, death threats, hate speech and misinformation, with indications of organized fake accounts; rumors on social media have been associated with communal violence (p. 13).

**Effect.** Adds scale and context for the slate's "the knowing was outside the company" framing; it does not change the earlier verdict.

## 9A. NHS NPfIT: PAC oral evidence of June 12, 2013 (selected questions)

Source: Public Accounts Committee oral evidence, Wednesday 12 June 2013, witnesses Sir David Nicholson (Chief Executive, NHS in England), Charlie Massey (Director General of External Relations, Department of Health) and Tim Donohoe (Senior Responsible Owner for Local Service Providers), https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm201314/cmselect/cmpubacc/294/130612.htm (read through the browser). I read Q1 to Q10, Q47 to Q50, Q142 to Q146 (in part), Q212 to Q215, and searched the rest for "accountab" and "Senior Responsible". About 106,000 characters; the rest is **not read**. Questions put by the Chair and Mr Bacon are members' statements, not findings.

1. **Was it closed? (Q1 to Q8).** The Chair said the September 2011 announcement that the programme was being closed "was not true". Sir David Nicholson replied the Government announced the programme was being taken apart as a nationally organised programme, with different governance, and that "there is no mechanism at the moment that brings it all together". The Chair called the change "deckchairs on the Titanic". Nicholson said the change meant a senior responsible officer for each major programme instead of strategic health authority chief executives being accountable geographically.
2. **Contracts continued (Q9 to Q10).** Mr Bacon pointed out that the same press release said existing contracts would be honoured; Mr Donohoe said the intention was to stop managing the national programme "as a single entity".
3. **Lorenzo (Q47 to Q50).** Mr Donohoe said Morecambe Bay was the key site where the system worked fully "with the exception of the parts of the software that have not yet been fully delivered" (the Committee report quotes the same words). Mr Bacon's Q50 describes the original contracts as bought on paper descriptions; that is his characterisation.
4. **Benefits monitoring (Q212 to Q215).** The Chair and Mr Bacon challenged the £10.7 billion forecast of benefits; Mr Donohoe said the Department was "not proposing to stop monitoring the benefits" and that "final" referred to the last presentation of the programmes as one portfolio.

**Effect.** Confirms the Committee report's account of the dismantling in the witnesses' own words and gives a quotable line on the accountability gap ("no mechanism" bringing it together). It does not change the verdict: the slate's framing (unreconciled information, split ownership) stands.

## 8C. Apollo 11: a flight controller's account (Garman, 2001)

Document: NASA Johnson Space Center Oral History Project, interview with John R. ("Jack") Garman by Kevin M. Rusnak, Houston, **March 27, 2001**, https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/garmanjr-3-27-01.pdf (the old historycollection.jsc.nasa.gov address now redirects to a NASA landing page). Garman was the back-room software support person for the guidance officer. This is **a recollection 32 years after the event**, not a record from 1969; I read printed transcript pp. 21 to 25 and searched the whole 67-page file for "1201", "1202", "alarm", "Bales" and "Kranz". The April 5, 2001 session was not read.

1. **The simulation.** Garman recalls that a few months before Apollo 11, "May or June" in his words, the guidance officer, Steve Bales, called an abort in a simulation for a computer alarm and "should not have"; the back room thought little of it (pp. 21 to 22).
2. **The response.** Flight Director Gene Kranz told them to write down every possible computer alarm, including ones that could not happen, and what each would mean; they did, in a handwritten list "under a piece of plastic" stuck under the glass on the console (p. 22).
3. **During the landing.** When an alarm came up, Bales asked what it was; they looked at the list; Garman told him on the back-room voice loop that "As long as it doesn't reoccur, it's fine" (p. 24). He explains the computer was self-cleaning through restarts. Bales looked at other data and gave his "We're go, flight" (p. 25).
4. **Second alarm.** When a different alarm of the same type came a few minutes later, Garman recalls yelling "Same type!", Bales repeating it and the CapCom repeating it (p. 25).
5. **Hindsight.** Garman says it was a witness moment where an abort call might have changed history, and that it was good Kranz and Bales kept their heads (pp. 25 to 26).
6. **Cause.** Garman says the reason for the overload was not known for a day or so after the landing (p. 23).

**Effect on the slate.** This is the first record I read in which a flight controller says what the controllers did and why, and it supports the slate's "recognized the alarm from a simulation" framing in the controllers' own words, as late recollection. It also qualifies it: the preparation was a list written after a mistaken abort in a simulation, and it covered alarms generally; Garman says the cause was not understood during the landing. The Mission Report's "decided before flight" wording (`receipts-ch7-9.md` receipt 6) and this recollection point the same way. It remains true that no 1969 controller-loop record was found, that the named controller still comes from commentary and later recollection, and that Garman's dates and wording are his memory.
