## 1. Columbia and Challenger: CAIB Chapter 8, "History as Cause: Columbia and Challenger"

### Document and how I read it

*Report of the Columbia Accident Investigation Board*, Volume I, August 2003, from `https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/caib-report.pdf`. Printed page = PDF page minus 1. **Read:** Chapter 8 pp. 195 to 197 (sections 8.1 and the start of 8.2), p. 197 to 198 (rest of the foam and O-ring signals), pp. 201 to 203 (the comparison of the two decision sequences), p. 203 (principles for change). **Not read:** sections 8.3, 8.4 and 8.6 in full, Chapters 5 and 7, and the other volumes. (The rest of Chapter 6 was read in `receipts-swaps.md`.)

### Receipts

1. **The Board pairs the two cases itself (p. 195).** The Board says the two investigations started with "remarkably similar" central questions: why NASA kept flying with known O-ring erosion (Challenger) or foam debris (Columbia), and why managers judged the conditions acceptable despite engineers' concerns. Board member Sally Ride, who also served on the Rogers Commission, observed "echoes" of Challenger in Columbia.
2. **Neither accident had a single cause (p. 195).** The foam hit was not the single cause of Columbia, just as the joint-seal failure was not the single cause of Challenger; both were also lost because of the failure of NASA's organizational system. The Board writes that "History is cause".
3. **"Normalization" (pp. 195 to 196).** The Board says the histories of engineering decisions on foam and O-rings had "identical trajectories" that "normalized" these anomalies so that flying with them became routine. In each case the first incident was accepted as tolerable, setting a precedent; later incidents were read as evidence the design was behaving as predicted; the engineering experience base worked like an "elastic waistband". Engineers "understood what was happening, but they never understood why".
4. **Weak versus strong signals (p. 197).** Foam strikes and O-ring erosion were frequent enough to become routine signals. In 1985 engineers recognized they needed data on cold temperature and O-ring erosion, but the task stayed on the back burner; the scorched putty was redefined after analysis as a weak signal. On Challenger's eve, engineers had no test data on cold. For Columbia, after the October 2002 STS-112 foam hit, the biggest foam hit to date was likewise redefined as a weak signal.
5. **The classification history, as the Board tells it (p. 196).** For Challenger it says the problematic joint "had been elevated" to Criticality 1, "later demoted" to Criticality 1-R, and then in the month before launch "closed out" of the problem-reporting system. **This differs from the Rogers Commission's account that I read earlier** (Volume I Chapter VI, pp. 125 to 128: the classification was changed from 1R to 1 on December 17, 1982; the Criticality 1 requirement was then waived on March 28, 1983; and later tracking documents still said 1-R). The two reports' wording about direction and sequence does not match in the passages I read; I did not resolve it. **Check both against the page text before scripting.**
6. **The comparison of the two decisions (pp. 201 to 202).** In both cases engineers first presented concerns plus possible solutions (an imagery request; a recommendation for temperature constraints) and "Management did not listen". For Columbia, program managers turned off the Kennedy engineers' initial request for Defense Department imagery, with apologies for not following "proper channels", and Debris Assessment Team analysts were asked to show a "mandatory need" without being told how. In both, engineers were asked to prove it was unsafe to fly, the reverse of the usual burden. Thiokol's Roger Boisjoly is quoted: "I had no data to quantify it."
7. **Who spoke and who did not (pp. 202 to 203).** At Challenger, some engineers with relevant information did not speak because of position, specialization or location; at the Thiokol off-line caucus managers voted and engineers did not; when a Marshall administrator asked "Does anyone have anything to add to this?", no one spoke. At Columbia, Debris Assessment Team members did not speak up when the Mission Management Team chair asked whether anyone had anything to add; they did not have numbers and were intimidated by hierarchy. The Board adds that astronauts were told in both cases that the problem was not a problem.
8. **Contractors (p. 202).** At Challenger, a Thiokol senior vice president told managers to "Take off your engineering hat and put on your management hat", and they reversed their engineers' position. At Columbia, NASA relied on Boeing's analysis with a tool used about 400 times outside its validated limits.
9. **Safety (p. 202).** No safety representatives were present at the Challenger teleconference; no one thought to call them. In Columbia's case safety representatives attended but were passive.
10. **The Board's conclusion on principles (p. 203).** Strategies must increase the "clarity, strength, and presence of signals that challenge assumptions about risk"; leaders create culture and are responsible for changing it. The Board also says its focus on context does not mean individuals are not accountable.

### What this does to the pairing decision

- **The Board's own text is a ready-made pairing.** Both investigations asked the same two questions; the Board frames both as failures of foresight in which normalized anomalies and a hierarchy that silenced dissent produced the loss (receipts 1 to 3, 6 to 7).
- **They can still take different angles.** From the sources read: *Challenger* is the case where the knowledge was recorded and formally acted on (a reclassification and waiver, a recommendation against launching, then a reversal in a management caucus); *Columbia* is the case where the knowledge was a concern and a request that were never allowed to become a decision (imagery turned off for "proper channels", the burden of proof reversed, a trusted expert's reassurance, a team that did not speak up).
- **Risks.** A viewer can feel they heard the story twice; NASA now supplies four of 30 slots (1B Mars Climate Orbiter, 3A Challenger, 7A Columbia, 8C Apollo 11), though 8C is deliberately a counter-example; and the two NASA reports disagree in wording on the O-ring classification history (receipt 5).

### Decision applied (2026-10-17, on the owner's go-ahead)

Keep both, positioned as a pair (3A in Chapter 3, 7A in Chapter 7), each with its own angle; do not release them back to back; settle the O-ring classification wording against the Rogers text before drafting 3A. Status: **READ** (Chapter 8 in part).

## 2. Flash Crash (2B)

### Document

*Findings Regarding the Market Events of May 6, 2010*, Report of the Staffs of the CFTC and SEC to the Joint Advisory Committee on Emerging Regulatory Issues, September 30, 2010, 104 pages. The SEC site refused curl; I downloaded the identical-titled copy from the CFTC site (`cftc.gov/.../staff-findings050610.pdf`). I re-read the Executive Summary's "Lessons Learned" (printed pp. 6 to 8). I **did not read any further body sections** in this pass: a search of the market-maker and high-frequency-trader sections (printed pp. 35 to 62) for pause or halt language returned little I could use, and I did not read those pages through. The earlier receipts (`receipts-ch1-3.md` §2B) remain the record for Executive Summary pages, §I.4, §II.2.a to b and §III.3.

### Receipts (printed pages)

1. **Automated execution without price limits (p. 6).** The report's first key lesson is that "under stressed market conditions" the automated execution of a large sell order can trigger extreme price movements, especially if the algorithm "does not take prices into account"; interaction between automated execution programs and algorithmic trading strategies can quickly erode liquidity.
2. **Volume is not liquidity (p. 6).** In times of significant volatility, "high trading volume is not necessarily a reliable indicator of market liquidity".
3. **Many private pause rules (p. 6).** Many market participants employ their own versions of a trading pause, based on different combinations of market signals. The withdrawal of one participant may not matter, but a liquidity crisis can develop if many withdraw at the same time, and in the extreme case trades can occur at stub quotes.
4. **A pause that helped (p. 6).** The CME's Stop Logic Functionality halted E-Mini trading and gave participants time to reassess; the report calls pausing a market an effective way to let algorithms reset.
5. **Cross-market links (p. 6).** The report calls May 6 "an important reminder of the inter-connectedness" of derivatives and securities markets, and says none of the market-wide circuit breakers triggered.
6. **Uncertainty about broken trades (p. 7).** Participants told staff they were concerned that only trades more than 60% from the reference price were broken, through a process that was not transparent; this uncertainty can affect willingness to provide liquidity.

### Effect on the reframe

- The reframe in the slate (the volume-only sell algorithm and each firm's own pause rule) matches the report's own lessons (receipts 1 to 3). The report does **not** say nobody could see the whole market; it does say the market is interconnected and that private pause rules interact (receipts 3 and 5).
- **Overlap with 2A (Knight Capital, already drafted).** Both are automated-trading failures in Chapter 2. The difference: Knight is one firm's deployment failure; the Flash Crash is many firms' separate sensible rules adding up.

### Decision applied

Adopt the reframe for 2B, titled and framed around the algorithm choice and the many private pause rules; keep the premise fit at "Partial". Coverage is still the Executive Summary plus four body sections of 104 pages.

## 3. Google Flu Trends (2C): Google's side

### Document and how I read it

Ginsberg, Mohebbi, Patel, Brammer, Smolinski and Brilliant, "Detecting influenza epidemics using search engine query data", *Nature* 457, 1012 to 1014 (February 19, 2009; online November 2008), doi 10.1038/nature07634. The Nature page is open access; I read the full text of the article body and Methods from `https://www.nature.com/articles/nature07634` (the supplementary files were not read). This is Google's own research paper, the original description of the system. I did **not** find or read Google's response to the 2014 critique, Google's later model updates, or its 2015 announcement that it would stop publishing estimates (the latter I know only from search-result summaries of news coverage, which say it stopped around August 2015 and gave data to research partners).

### Receipts

1. **The method is described in the paper.** Weekly counts for "50 million of the most common search queries" for 2003 to 2008; each tested as a single-variable model against CDC influenza-like-illness (ILI) data; 450 million models fitted in total; the top 45 queries, selected automatically, were summed into the final predictor (Methods; Results).
2. **The numbers Lazer and colleagues cite are in Google's paper.** The final model was regressed over 1,152 points (128 training weeks in each of nine regions) and validated on 42 further weeks per region; mean correlation 0.90 on the fit and 0.97 on validation (Results; Online Methods).
3. **The paper reports that odd queries scored well.** Other top-100 queries not included in the model included topics like "high school basketball", which coincide with flu season; Figure 1's caption notes a steep fall in performance after query 81, "oscar nominations".
4. **The paper names its own failure modes.**
   - "panic and concern among healthy individuals may cause a surge in the ILI-related query fraction and exaggerated estimates".
   - Unusual events such as a drug recall could cause a false alert.
   - Correlations are meaningful only across large populations.
   - The system "is not designed to be a replacement" for traditional surveillance, and the data are most useful to spur further investigation.
5. **What it claims.** Estimates were 1 to 2 weeks ahead of CDC reports; the authors say the approach may become an important line of defence against future epidemics (Results; Discussion).
6. **What it does not publish.** The paper gives topics of the selected queries (Table 1) but I did not see the list of the 45 queries themselves in the text I read; the system's results were to be freely available online at Google's flu trends address (Discussion).

### Effect on the framing

- **The slate's reframe does not hold as worded.** "A maker that kept changing it and never published how it worked" is partly contradicted by receipts 1 and 2: the method and the key counts were published. Receipt 6 (no query list) supports only a narrower version.
- **A different thread is supported by Google's own paper:** the authors wrote down how the system could fail (receipt 4), and the 2014 critique reports that it later failed in a related way. Whether the original warning travelled with the product after 2009 is something I could not check; I did not read Google's later communication or the system's public pages.
- **Premise fit stays "Weak to partial."** The evidence on what the later estimates did comes from the critics' paper (read earlier), not from Google.

### Decision applied

**Do not adopt the "never published how it worked" reframe.** Keep 2C in the slate with its fit at "Weak to partial", record the alternative thread (the authors' own stated failure modes), and mark the slot as the next one to swap if the owner wants a stronger Chapter 2 episode. I have no swap candidate that has been read.
