### 2. Receipts

1. **What BSR was and was not.** HRIA by BSR between May and September 2018, method based on the UN Guiding Principles; about 60 rightsholders and stakeholders consulted over two visits to Myanmar, plus Facebook employees; funded by Facebook, "BSR retained editorial control". (About This Report, p.2; Exec. Summary p.4; §2.1 p.7.) BSR says it is not an audit, not "a fact-finding mission regarding human rights violations", and that assessing "the extent, completeness, or effectiveness of implementation" of Facebook policies was outside its scope (§2.1, p.7).
2. **Where "not doing enough" comes from.** Facebook's post says the report "concludes that, prior to this year, we weren't doing enough to help prevent our platform" from being used to incite offline violence, and "We agree that we can and should do more." (Post, third and fourth paragraphs.) That sentence is Facebook's description. I searched the BSR text and did not find it there. The closest BSR wording is in §5.3 (p.30): many stakeholders, and Facebook itself, emphasized that previous efforts "have fallen short". On p.29 BSR says Facebook faces a significant trust deficit in Myanmar because of actual or perceived lack of sufficient action on rising hate speech.
3. **No local presence.** "Facebook does not have a physical presence" in Myanmar: no staff, offices or data (§2.2, p.8). About 20 million Facebook users in Myanmar (Security table, p.36). "Facebook is the internet" is described as a common saying (§5.1, p.24).
4. **What BSR says Facebook's role was.** For the Security risks (hate speech, incitement, coordinated harm), the table says Facebook "does not cause or contribute to these risks via its own actions" and is instead directly linked to them via users' violations of Community Standards (Security table, p.36; framework §6.1, pp.34-35). BSR also says the actual relationship between posts and offline harm is not fully understood, while posts have been linked to offline violence, citing the UN mission (§5.1, p.25).
5. **Local language and context capacity.** BSR reports that in August 2018 Facebook said it employed 60 Myanmar-language reviewers and expected at least 100 by end of 2018 (§4.2, p.22), and that proactive detection of hate-speech removals went from 13% (Q4 2017) to 52% (Q2 2018) (p.22). It records "substantial consensus", including among Facebook's own staff, that enforcement in Myanmar needs Burmese staff with insight into cultural, political, conflict, ethnic, religious and language factors (§5.2, p.27). Stakeholder skepticism about AI coping with Burmese is recorded (p.29, drawing on the interviewees' "lack of data" concern).
6. **Standards vs. enforcement.** Interviewees found the Community Standards largely fit for purpose; one is quoted: "The Community Standards are mostly fine, but they are not being enforced well enough." (§5.2, p.27.) BSR also records that local suggestions (e.g. letting reviewers see the whole comment thread) were ones where it was clear Facebook was already aware or had different processes in place (§5.2, p.28).
7. **How long the warnings had been going.** BSR says civil society organizations have been raising concerns with Facebook "going back to 2012" (§5.3, pp.29-30). An interviewee is quoted: "After four years of calls for action" Facebook only began to act after a civil society letter drew attention (p.29). That is a stakeholder's view, reported by BSR, not a BSR finding.
8. **BSR recommendations.** *Governance:* create a stand-alone human rights policy; "deepen and formalize" a leadership, governance and accountability structure using existing mechanisms (Audit and Risk Oversight Committee, Content Standards Forum, executive roles) rather than a parallel one; publish periodic human rights updates; keep doing HRIAs in other high-risk markets (Exec. Summary p.6; §7.1, pp.43-44; §4.1 p.21 notes Facebook then had no holistic human rights policy). *Enforcement and language:* build a cross-functional local-context team (ethnicity, languages, different Burmese dialects, 24/7 coverage, a small portion eventually in Myanmar); lower the threshold for misinformation that may exacerbate violence; draw on local stakeholders systematically; keep investing in AI; research how hate speech spreads; fund local organizations (§7.2, pp.46-48; Exec. Summary pp.6-7). *Platform:* join the Unicode transition; over 90 percent of Myanmar phones use Zawgyi, so a Zawgyi phone cannot properly read Unicode text (§7.2, p.52).
9. **What Facebook itself said (Nov 5, 2018).** It hired and onboarded 99 of its planned 100 native-language reviewers; set up a dedicated Myanmar team across product, engineering and policy; updated its credible violence policy to remove misinformation that may contribute to imminent violence; took action on about 64,000 pieces of content in Q3 2018 for hate speech, of which 63% were proactively identified, "up from 13%" in Q4 2017 and 52% in Q2 2018. On Zawgyi it says the lack of a single standard makes "automation and proactive detection of bad content harder". It also says "Facebook alone cannot bring about the broad changes needed". (Post, sections on Enforcement and Advocacy.) These are Facebook's own figures; I did not check them against Facebook's transparency reports.
10. **UN Mission on Facebook's role and response.** A/HRC/39/64 ¶74: Facebook "has been a useful instrument for those seeking to spread hate", in a context where for most users Facebook is the Internet; although improved in recent months, the response of Facebook has been "slow and ineffective"; the extent to which posts led to real-world discrimination and violence must be independently and thoroughly examined; the Mission regrets that Facebook could not provide country-specific data on hate speech. CRP.2 ¶¶1346-1354 say the same at greater length. ¶1354: the Mission has "no doubt" that the prevalence of hate speech in Myanmar significantly contributed to tension and a climate receptive to incitement, which also applies to hate speech on Facebook, while the extent of Facebook's contribution still needs independent research. The phrase "determining role", often attributed to the Mission, is **not** in either document (searched).
11. **The Mission's own test.** CRP.2 ¶1351 (printed pp.341-342): the Mission reported a post calling for the killing of a human rights defender four times through the standard mechanism; each reply said the post "doesn't go against one of [Facebook's] specific Community Standards"; an email to an official Facebook address got no reply; the post came down several weeks later only through a contact at Facebook, not the official channel; months later at least 16 re-posts were still circulating.
12. **Civil society and Facebook, as the Mission records them.** ¶1350: six local civil society groups wrote to Facebook (open letter, April 5, 2018, not read) describing "an over-reliance on third parties" and a lack of a mechanism for "emergency escalation", plus concerns about too few moderators who understand the language and context and about font incompatibility. ¶1352 and fn 2991: Facebook had publicly acknowledged being "too slow" in reacting to civil society concerns (the footnote cites Zuckerberg's April 2018 congressional testimony; not read); in June 2018 Facebook told the Mission it was working on fonts, reporting flows, automation and misinformation and would commission an HRIA. ¶1353: in 2018 Facebook designated MaBaTha and three monks as hate figures; on Aug 27, 2018 it said it had removed 18 Facebook accounts, one Instagram account and 52 Pages and banned 20 individuals and organizations including Senior-General Min Aung Hlaing.

### 3. Does the source support the slate?

- **Slate's factual description: supported, with one correction of source.** The slate says BSR's May-to-September 2018 assessment, published Nov 2018, funded by Facebook with BSR keeping editorial control, "concluded Facebook had not done enough before 2018", per Facebook's own post (receipts 1, 2). That conclusion is Facebook's characterization; I did not find it in the BSR text (receipt 2). The slate said the UN findings were not located; they are now read (receipts 10-12). The title "Too Slow" is supported by the Mission (¶¶74, 1351) and by Facebook's own acknowledgement (¶1352, post).
- **The slate has no proposed angle for 5C.** If the angle is "where the knowing sat": **partly supported, and the premise needs care.** The documents do not show that nobody held the knowing. They show the opposite for the warnings: local civil society had been raising concerns since 2012 (receipt 7), the Mission's four reports of a post calling for a killing were met with "doesn't go against" replies (receipt 11), and BSR found Facebook was already aware of specific local suggestions (receipt 6). What the documents support is a gap between that outside knowledge and Facebook's capacity to act on it in Burmese: no in-country presence (receipt 3), a Burmese-language reviewer team of about 60 in August 2018 against about 20 million users (receipts 3, 5), Zawgyi font incompatibility with Facebook's tools (receipts 8, 9), no emergency escalation mechanism per the civil society letter as quoted by the UN (receipt 12). That is a "knowing that existed outside the company and did not reach enforcement" story, not "nobody held it".
- **Limits an episode must respect.** Neither document establishes how much Facebook content caused violence: BSR p.25 and CRP.2 ¶1354 both say that is not established or needs independent study. BSR classifies Facebook as "linked", not "cause or contribute", for the security risks (p.36). BSR did not assess effectiveness of implementation (p.7). The Facebook figures are Facebook's.
- **Fits the channel premise?** Partly. It fits if framed as "the people who knew were outside the building". No swap needed, but the episode should not say "nobody knew".

Nothing in it can be checked against these documents, so there is no conflict and no overlap to cite. The slate's note ("Content moderation is a theme; Myanmar is not named") is accurate.

### 2. Receipts

Everything from the complaint is **an allegation by the plaintiff**, not a finding. The City's answer mostly denies or says it lacks knowledge (receipt 7).

1. **The theft and the footage.** A man stole five watches from a Shinola store in Detroit on Oct 2, 2018, in about two minutes on camera. The footage never shows the face clearly: back turned most of the time, a baseball cap, face partly visible from above and afar. At least one salesperson spoke to him face to face for about 25 seconds. (FAC ¶¶4, 56-62.)
2. **First step was facial recognition, with no DPD policy.** DPD's investigation sat for over five months. On March 8, 2019 a DPD investigator sent a still from the footage for a facial recognition search, via a DPD analyst to Michigan State Police. DPD had no policy governing facial recognition until April 2019. (FAC ¶¶66-67, 75-77, 209-210.) Then-Chief Craig is quoted as conceding the image "is blurry" (¶71).
3. **The lead report.** MSP returned an "Investigative Lead Report" on March 11, 2019 naming Williams. (FAC ¶78; MSP report, ECF 60-1.) The report as I read it shows a dark probe image and a licence-style photo of Williams, a digital image examiner and the requesting analyst named, and a banner in red and black: "THIS DOCUMENT IS NOT A POSITIVE IDENTIFICATION", an "INVESTIGATIVE LEAD ONLY", "NOT PROBABLE CAUSE TO ARREST", further investigation needed. The page shows no confidence score and no other candidates; the complaint says the same (¶81) and that DPD never asked for them (¶82). The matched photo was an **expired** driver's licence photo; his newer photo did not come up as a likely match (¶79).
4. **What the detective did.** Detective Bussa took the case on May 14, 2019 (¶84). Per the complaint he did not check Williams's whereabouts, took no witness statements, collected no fingerprints or DNA (¶8); never asked MSP how the lead was generated or about its confidence (¶83); and was not trained on how facial recognition works (¶83). He built a six-photo array using Williams's *current* licence photo (¶88), failed to get actual store eyewitnesses to take part (¶¶89-91), and on July 30, 2019 ran the lineup with Katherine Johnston, a loss-prevention contractor who was not in the store and had only watched the same video (¶¶92-95). The lineup was not blind, she had been told there was a facial recognition "match", she looked at a video still while choosing, and the complaint says no confidence level or recording was made (¶¶8, 96-98).
5. **The warrant request.** Same day, Bussa wrote the request for warrant (¶100). The complaint alleges it called Johnston's pick an identification without saying she was not an eyewitness, did not say she knew of the facial recognition match, called the search a "hit", and left out that the probe was blurry and the matched photo was expired and that no confidence score was given (¶¶104-113). The video was not given to the prosecutor or magistrate (¶¶118, 121). Supervisory approval came from a sergeant outside Bussa's chain of command who the complaint says had no investigative training (¶¶114-116). Prosecutor's office authorized a warrant on Aug 24, 2019; the magistrate was presented with it Aug 25 (¶¶119-120).
6. **The arrest, after a rule change.** DPD adopted Manual Directive 307.5 on Sept 19, 2019, limiting facial recognition to active violent-crime or home-invasion investigations, with peer review (¶¶230-233); the complaint notes Williams's arrest on **Jan 9, 2020** came over three months after that policy, which barred facial recognition for non-violent theft (¶130) and that it had no retroactive review of existing warrants (¶234). Police arrested him in his driveway at about 5:22 p.m., his two young daughters (5 and 2) watching and his wife also present (¶¶146-149). He first learned the accusation when he demanded to see the warrant (¶¶149-150).
7. **How long he was held.** The complaint gives the figure four times: "approximately 30 hours" in the introduction (¶1), and again in ¶¶3, 154 and 188. Its own timeline: arrested about 5:22 p.m. Jan 9; DNA and palm prints taken about 1:00 a.m. (¶¶161-162); video arraignment the next morning (¶169); interrogation after the plea, during which officers realized he was not the person in the images (¶¶171-182); released on a personal bond after about eight more hours (¶186). The City's answer to ¶¶3, 154, 186 and 188 says it lacks knowledge and so denies. **So 30 hours is the plaintiff's account, and I found no jail record or admission of it in the documents I read.**
8. **Charges.** Jan 23, 2020: the prosecutor's office dismissed all charges, "without prejudice", with the right to refile (¶201).
9. **What city and DPD officials said (as quoted in the complaint).** At the July 9, 2020 Board of Police Commissioners meeting, the City's corporation counsel said the City would be "conceding liability" (¶236); Chief Craig called the work "clearly sloppy, sloppy investigative work" (¶244) and said that had the September 2019 policy been earlier, "I am convinced [the wrongful arrest] would not have happened." (¶240). The complaint quotes Craig saying that if a department relied only on the technology, it would misidentify (¶238), and the Assistant Chief on weaknesses in the April 2019 policies (¶242). The City's answer says it lacks knowledge of those paragraphs (e.g. ¶244). I did not read the meeting transcript or video.
10. **Settlement terms (June 28, 2024).** Case dismissed with prejudice; court retains jurisdiction to enforce for four years (Order ¶¶2-3). Settlement Agreement: the agreement is "not to be construed as an admission of liability" (§2); DPD to implement and enforce revised Manual Directive 307.5 (§3), investigative-lead and vetting forms (§4), and the changed provisions of Directive 203.11 on eyewitness identification and lineups (§5); an audit within 180 days of all cases since Feb 22, 2017 (through Aug 10, 2023) where a facial recognition lead was followed by an arrest or warrant, checking for independent evidence and notifying the prosecutor where there is none (§6); a training program for four years (§7); no weakening of the protections for four years, including no lineup or warrant request resting only on a facial recognition lead or such a lineup (§8). The ACLU one-pager adds: a lineup may never be based solely on a facial recognition lead, and a lead plus a lineup identification is never enough to seek a warrant; detectives must document independent steps and get two supervisors' sign-off; witnesses may not be told that facial recognition identified anyone; lineups must be sequential.
11. **The amount.** Not in any court document I read: Attachment E is redacted in its entirety (Agreement §9, pp.4-5 of the exhibit). The ACLU one-pager and press release say only that Detroit will pay "an agreed amount" or "monetary damages" and attorneys' fees. **$300,000 appears in the AP report** ("agreed to pay $300,000 to a man..."); I could not tell from the text I read what source AP relied on, or whether the figure covers fees. Treat $300,000 as press-reported only.
12. **Nijeer Parks.** Primary document found: the Amended Complaint in *Parks v. McCormac* (D.N.J. No. 2:21-cv-04021), filed Oct 7, 2022, plus a docket (CourtListener) showing the case filed March 3, 2021 and terminated July 9, 2024 (docket metadata; I did not read the outcome). From the complaint pages I read (allegations only): suspect left a fake Tennessee licence and other items at the Hampton Inn in Woodbridge on Jan 26, 2019 and drove at an officer (Facts ¶¶1-6). The photo **from the fake licence** was sent for facial recognition; this "resulted in" a "high profile" match to Parks (¶¶23-24); no lineup of that match, no fingerprint or DNA check of Parks despite prints lifted at the scene (¶¶12-16, 25, 28). The complaint alleges fingerprints identified a different man, Barrington Walker, and that this was hidden until discovery (¶16, Introduction). Parks, who is alleged to be 5'7" and to have held no driver's licence at the time (¶¶36, 54), went to Woodbridge on about Feb 5, 2019 to clear a warrant and was arrested (¶¶57-61). First court appearance "three days after" arrest (¶84); a second "about a week later" (¶86); held in intake a week (¶76); transfer to a tier "seven days later" (¶81). **The pages I read do not state a total of ten days.** I did not read pages 16-29.

### 3. Does the source support the slate?

- **Slate's factual description: supported.** Williams was arrested in 2020 after a facial recognition match connected to a 2018 theft (receipts 1, 3, 6). The settlement bars arrests based solely on facial recognition results or on photo lineups generated from them, requires training, and requires an audit of warrants since 2017 (receipt 10). The slate said the figure was $300,000 per ACLU and news; it is press-reported only (receipt 11). The ACLU summary says "agreed amount".

The probe's poor quality (analyst, detective), the expired photo and missing confidence score (MSP and DPD), that the lineup witness was not present and was told of the "match" (Bussa), and the rule against facial recognition for non-violent theft (policy adopted more than three months before the arrest, ¶130) were all held somewhere in the system, and the request for warrant is alleged to have omitted them (receipts 3-6). Caveat: all of this is the plaintiff's account; the City's answer denies or says it lacks knowledge of most paragraphs, and the officials' statements are read only as quoted by the complaint (receipt 9).
- **Fits the channel premise?** Yes, with the allegation caveat. No swap needed.

### 2. Receipts

1. **Dataset.** The Pilot Parliaments Benchmark (PPB): 1,270 unique individuals, parliamentarians from three African countries (Rwanda, Senegal, South Africa) and three European (Iceland, Finland, Sweden), picked for gender parity in national parliaments. (§1 p.2; §3.3 p.5; Table 1 p.5; Table 2 p.7.) Images are official profile photos, constrained pose and lighting (§3.3, p.6); the authors call their sample "optimistic" and say errors would be higher on unconstrained images (§4.7, p.12).
2. **How it was labeled.** Skin type on the six-point Fitzpatrick scale: lighter = types I-III, darker = IV-VI (§3.5, p.7). Three annotators including the authors labeled PPB, and a board-certified surgical dermatologist provided "the definitive labels" (§3.4, p.6; named in Acknowledgments, p.12). Gender labels came from name, title and the appearance of the photo, and the paper stresses they are binary labels for "perceived" women and men (§3.3-3.4, p.6).
3. **PPB composition.** Darker female 21.3%, darker male 25.0%, lighter female 23.3%, lighter male 30.3% (Table 2, p.7; Figure 3, p.6). Overall 46.4% darker, 53.6% lighter (Table 3, p.7).
4. **The benchmark skew figures.** IJB-A (US-government benchmark, 500 subjects): 79.6% lighter-skinned. Adience (2,194 labeled subjects): 86.2% lighter-skinned. (Abstract; Table 3, p.7.) By group (Figure 3, p.6, read from the image): IJB-A darker female 4.4%, darker male 16.0%, lighter female 20.2%, lighter male 59.4%; Adience 7.4%, 6.4%, 44.6%, 41.6%. The text (§3.5, p.7) matches: darker females are least represented in IJB-A (4.4%), darker males least in Adience (6.4%). **The Figure 3 caption says "Only 4.4% of subjects in Adience are darker-skinned and female"; the figure and the text put 4.4% on IJB-A.** The caption looks like an error in the paper; don't quote the caption.
5. **The three vendors.** Microsoft (Cognitive Services Face API), IBM (Watson Visual Recognition), and Face++ (Megvii, China). Evaluated April and May 2017 each offered a public demonstration at the time. Google was not tested because it had no public gender classifier at the time. (§4.2, p.8; Table 4, p.9.)
6. **What the vendors disclosed.** The paper says their documentation lacked methodology detail and did not say what training data was used; none reported performance on existing gender benchmarks; Face++'s terms disclaim accuracy warranties; only IBM returned confidence scores (§4.2, p.8). It also says the companies gave no documentation on whether their labels reflect gender identity or biological sex (§3.3, p.6).
7. **The exact error rates (Table 4, p.9, PPB, viewed as image).**

   All values are error rates in %. **34.7% is IBM's error on darker females; 0.8% is Face++'s error on lighter males** (the abstract's "up to 34.7%" for darker-skinned females and "maximum error rate for lighter-skinned males is 0.8%"). Face++ is 34.5% on darker females. Per §4.1 (p.8): Microsoft and IBM do best on lighter males (0.0% and 0.3%); Face++ does best on darker males (0.7%); the largest gap between best and worst groups is 34.4%.
8. **Aggregates hide it.** Overall accuracy of 87.9% to 93.7% on PPB would suggest the classifiers "can be used for all populations", yet error is 8.1-20.6 points worse for women than men and 11.8-19.2 points worse for darker than lighter faces (§4.5, p.11; §4.1, p.8). Darker females are 21.3% of PPB but make up 61.0% to 72.4% of the errors; lighter males are 30.3% of PPB and contribute 0.0% to 2.4% of errors (§4.4, p.10).
9. **South African subset.** Because South Africa's parliament has comparable image quality and the widest skin-type spread, the authors use it to test whether image quality explains the gaps. Table 5 (p.9): darker-female error was 23.8% (Microsoft), 36.0% (Face++), 33.1% (IBM). They conclude image characteristics do not "fully account" for the gaps (§4.4, p.10).
10. **What the paper says about causes.** It says darker skin may be "highly correlated with facial geometries or gender display norms" that were less represented in the training data (§4.4, p.10). That is a hedged hypothesis. The paper had no vendor training data to examine (receipt 6). The skew figures (receipt 4) are about the **public benchmarks** IJB-A and Adience, not vendor training sets.
11. **Vendor response: none in this paper.** The paper has no section on vendor response (searched "response", "notified", "updated"; the only hit was an unrelated "In response to these limitations"). **The follow-up paper** (Raji and Buolamwini, AIES 2019) says the Gender Shades paper, without company names, was sent to Microsoft, IBM and Face++ on Dec 19, 2017 (p.2); it was publicized with names in February 2018; all three released new API versions, IBM after 66 days, Microsoft after 189, Face++ after 190 (p.4). In an Aug 21, 2018 re-audit on PPB, darker-female error was Face++ 4.1%, Microsoft 1.52%, IBM 16.97%; lighter-male error was 0.5%, 0.00%, 0.26% (Table 1, p.4). Two vendors that were not in the original audit, Amazon and Kairos, scored 31.37% and 22.50% on darker females.
12. **What the paper says should follow.** Its definitions: transparency means providing the demographic and phenotypic composition of training and benchmark datasets; accountability means reporting performance by subgroup and "actively working to close performance gaps" (Conclusion, p.12). It says the gender-classification results may not carry over to other face tasks (p.12).

## 7B. Ariane 5 Flight 501 (Chapter 7, slot B)

### 1. Document and how it was read

**"ARIANE 5, Flight 501 Failure, Report by the Inquiry Board", Paris, 19 July 1996, Chairman Prof. J. L. Lions.** Board members from Academie des Sciences, Swedish Space Corporation, DGA, INRIA, TU Darmstadt, Daimler Benz Aerospace, Alenia Spazio, Thomson CSF and DERA (Foreword, p.1). Copy at https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-355j-software-engineering-concepts-fall-2005/91f1e550b30b00ad797293f430220f18_ari5fail_ful_rep.pdf (15 pages, a 2006 web capture). Downloaded; pdftotext; **read the whole text, all 607 lines, from the Foreword to Recommendation R14.** Locators: report section numbers, then the PDF page. Not read: the separate "Technical Report" for restricted circulation that the Foreword mentions.

### 2. Receipts

1. **The loss.** The maiden flight, 4 June 1996: about 40 seconds after the flight sequence began, at about 3,700 m, "the launcher veered off its flight path, broke up and exploded." Nominal until about H0+36 s; self-destruct triggered correctly by rupture of the booster links. (Foreword p.1; §1.1, pp.2-3.)
2. **Chain of failure, traced backward.** Disintegration at about H0+39 s from aerodynamic loads at an angle of attack above 20 degrees; caused by full nozzle deflections commanded by the On-Board Computer from data sent by the active Inertial Reference System (SRI 2), which was in fact sending a diagnostic bit pattern interpreted as flight data; SRI 2 had stopped because of a software exception; the back-up SRI 1 had stopped one 72-ms data cycle earlier for the same reason. (§2.1, pp.4-5; findings e-g, §3.1, pp.11-12.) Times: back-up unit at H0+36.7 s, active unit about 0.05 s later (findings e, f).
3. **The exception.** A conversion in Ada code of a 64-bit floating-point value to a 16-bit signed integer: the value was larger than 16 bits can hold, giving an Operand Error. The conversion was unprotected, although other conversions of comparable variables in the same place in the code were protected. The variable was **BH, "Horizontal Bias"**, an internal alignment result related to the horizontal velocity sensed by the platform. (§2.1, p.5; §2.2, p.6.)
4. **Where it ran.** In the part of the software that only aligns the strap-down platform before lift-off; the function "serves no purpose" after lift-off yet kept running for about 40 seconds of flight (50 s after flight mode starts at H0-3 s). That timing "is based on a requirement of Ariane 4 and is not required for Ariane 5". (§2.1, p.5.)
5. **The reuse, and the reasons the report gives.** The Ariane 5 SRI design "is practically the same" as the one used on Ariane 4, particularly the software (§2.1, p.4). The alignment-after-lift-off requirement dated from more than 10 years earlier, for a hold in the countdown between -9 s and -5 s, letting the count restart without a 45-minute realignment; it was used once, in 1989 on Flight 33. Ariane 5 has a different preparation sequence, and the function was kept "for commonality reasons", "presumably" on the view that unproven changes to software that worked on Ariane 4 were unwise. (§2.2, p.7; finding m, p.12.) "Presumably" is the Board's inference, not a documented decision.
6. **Why that variable was unprotected.** The Board was told not all conversions were protected because of an 80% maximum workload target for the SRI computer. An analysis of every operation that could raise an exception found seven at-risk variables; four were protected and three, including BH, were left unprotected because further reasoning judged them physically limited or to have "a large margin of safety", reasoning the Board says was faulty for BH. No justification appears in the source code, and "the assumption, although agreed, was essentially obscured, though not deliberately, from any external review". The decision to protect some variables and not others was taken jointly by project partners at several contractual levels. (§2.2, p.6.)
7. **The Ariane 5 trajectory was not in the spec.** "There is no evidence that any trajectory data were used" to analyse the unprotected variables, and it was jointly agreed not to include Ariane 5 trajectory data in the SRI requirements and specification (§2.2, p.6). The early trajectory differs and gives a horizontal velocity build-up "five times more rapid than for Ariane 4"; on Ariane 4 the variable cannot reach the limit with adequate margin (findings o, p, §3.1, pp.12-13).
8. **The shutdown rule.** The system specification said that on any exception the failure is indicated on the bus, context stored in EEPROM, and the processor shut down. The Board says it was the decision to cease processor operation "which finally proved fatal", reflecting a programme culture of "only addressing random hardware failures", where a backup is a sound answer. The same software ran in both units. (§2.2, pp.6-7.) "The supplier of the SRI was only following the specification given to it" (§2.2, p.7).
9. **What was and was not tested.** SRI equipment-level testing was rigorous, "beyond what was expected for Ariane 5", but "no test was performed to verify that the SRI would behave correctly" under the Ariane 5 countdown, flight-time sequence and trajectory. A ground test injecting simulated accelerometer signals with a turntable was possible; had it been done, "the failure mechanism would have been exposed". The reason given: the SRI spec lacks the Ariane 5 trajectory, and does not state operational restrictions from the implementation. (§2.3, p.8.)
10. **The simulation decision.** In the closed-loop tests at the Functional Simulation Facility the two SRIs were simulated by software modules; the open-loop tests with the real SRI were electrical and bus-level compliance tests only. Originally the real SRI was to be in the loop; in **1992** that was changed, for four reasons: the SRI was considered fully qualified at equipment level, the test electronics could not match the SRI's precision, failure modes can only be simulated with a model, and the SRI's 1 ms base period differed from the facility's 6 ms. The Board calls those arguments "technically valid" but says there was "a definite risk" in assuming a critical item such as the SRI had been validated by its own qualification or by previous use on Ariane 4. Finding s: it was technically feasible to include almost the entire SRI in the simulations, and the Board says the failure could have been detected had it been. (§2.3, pp.8-10; finding s, p.13.)
11. **Reviews.** The Board says the review process involves all major partners and external experts and handles literally thousands of problems, yet the limitations of the SRI software "were not fully analysed", the inadequate test coverage was not realised, and the implications of letting the alignment software run in flight were not realised; in these respects the review process was "a contributory factor" in the failure. (§2.3, pp.10-11; finding q, p.12.) Official cause (§3.2, p.13): complete loss of guidance and attitude information 37 s after main engine ignition, "specification and design errors in the software of the inertial reference system".
12. **Recommendations (§4, pp.14-15).** R1: switch off the alignment function right after lift-off; "no software function should run during flight unless it is needed". R2: a closed-loop test facility with as much real equipment as feasible. R3: no sensor may stop sending best-effort data. R4: a software qualification review for each equipment item, with the Industrial Architect taking part, all restrictions on use made explicit, and critical software made a Configuration Controlled Item. R5: review all flight software and identify the implicit assumptions the code makes about equipment values. R9: include reviewers from outside the project who weigh the substance of arguments rather than check that verifications were made. R10: trajectory data in specifications and test requirements. R12: give justification documents the same attention as code. R14: more transparent cooperation among the partners, with "clear cut authority and responsibility".

**Who held which knowledge, as the report writes it.** The report names no individuals or companies for these roles; it uses "the supplier of the SRI", "the Industrial Architect" (the site of the simulation facility, §2.3 p.8), "project partners at several contractual levels" and "the Board". It says: the SRI supplier followed the specification (receipt 8); the specification lacked the Ariane 5 trajectory (7); the BH decision was joint and "obscured" from external review (6); the reviews involving all major partners did not catch it (11). **Not stated in the report:** the cost, the payload, any person's name or any firm's name in a fault role, and whether anyone at any firm knew the BH limit would be exceeded on Ariane 5.

### 3. Does the source support the slate?

- **Slate's factual description: supported.** The slate says the Board "traces the loss to specification and design errors in the inertial reference system's software and to inadequate testing", and that the code was reused from Ariane 4. Receipts 3-5, 9-11 confirm all three; the "secondary summaries" the slate relied on for the reuse are in line with the report itself (receipt 5).
- **Angle: "knowledge of an assumption (the flight envelope) stayed with a different rocket": partly supported.** The report supports that the validity of the unprotected variable depended on Ariane 4's trajectory (receipt 7), that the requirement for post-lift-off alignment came from Ariane 4 and was kept for commonality (receipt 5), and that the limitation was not in the Ariane 5 specification (receipt 7). But it does not say anyone *held* the knowledge and failed to pass it on. It says the limitation "was not analysed or fully understood" (finding n) and was agreed but "obscured" in documentation. Better framing from the text: the assumption's justification was never written into the requirements where Ariane 5's engineers and reviewers would meet it.
- **Fits the channel premise?** Yes: it is among the strongest fits in my five. No swap needed. One caution: the report praises the programme's "high quality" engineering and documentation (Foreword, p.2) and that is part of the story.

### 4. Differences from the book

None to report. Chapters 1-9 and X contain no mention of Ariane (searched); the slate's "No" is correct.
